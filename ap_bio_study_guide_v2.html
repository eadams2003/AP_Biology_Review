The 4 Big Ideas — Spiraling Throughout All Units
Big Idea 1 — Evolution: The process of evolution drives the diversity and unity of life. Natural selection is the major mechanism.
Big Idea 2 — Energetics: Biological systems use energy and molecular building blocks to grow, reproduce, and maintain dynamic homeostasis.
Big Idea 3 — Information Storage & Transmission: Living systems store, retrieve, transmit, and respond to information essential to life processes.
Big Idea 4 — Systems Interactions: Biological systems interact, and these systems and their interactions exhibit complex properties.

Unit 1 — Chemistry of Life

Sets the chemical foundation for all of biology. Covers water's unique properties, the elements of life, and the four macromolecules. Structure determines function — this theme runs through the entire course.

Topic 1.1 — Water & Hydrogen Bonding
  • Polarity: The O–H bonds in water are polar covalent — oxygen pulls electrons more strongly (higher electronegativity), creating a partial negative charge (δ−) on O and partial positive charges (δ+) on each H. This dipole allows water molecules to attract each other via hydrogen bonds.
  • Hydrogen bonds: Individually weak (~5% the strength of a covalent bond) but collectively very strong due to the enormous number that form simultaneously. H-bonds form between the δ+ hydrogen of one water molecule and the δ− oxygen of an adjacent molecule.
  • High specific heat (1 cal/g°C): Water absorbs a large amount of heat before its temperature rises, because energy first breaks H-bonds rather than increasing molecular motion. This stabilizes body temperatures and moderates climate near large bodies of water.
  • High heat of vaporization (586 cal/g): Evaporation requires breaking H-bonds, which absorbs heat from the surface — this is the basis of evaporative cooling (sweating, panting). Very efficient for temperature regulation.
  • Cohesion: H-bonds between water molecules make them "stick" to each other → responsible for surface tension. Water striders, needles, and paper clips can sit on water because of cohesion at the surface.
  • Adhesion: Water sticks to polar and charged surfaces (glass, cellulose in plant cell walls) via H-bonds → combined with cohesion, drives capillary action — water moving up against gravity in narrow tubes (e.g., plant xylem).
  • Universal solvent: Water's polarity allows it to surround and dissolve ionic compounds and polar molecules (hydrophilic), enabling transport of nutrients, gases, and waste in biological systems.
  • Ice is less dense than liquid water: H-bonds in ice form a stable crystalline lattice that is less dense than liquid water — ice floats, insulating aquatic life below.
MCQ Insight (Q54): Bile salts decrease surface tension by disrupting hydrogen bonds — sulfur powder sinks in urine with high bile salts. Know that surface tension = cohesion of water molecules at the surface.
Topic 1.2 & 1.3 — Elements & Macromolecules
  • Major elements: C, H, O make up most of every macromolecule. Nitrogen (N) is added in proteins and nucleic acids. Phosphorus (P) appears in nucleic acids and phospholipids. Sulfur (S) is in some amino acids (cysteine, methionine) and forms disulfide bridges.
  • Carbon's unique role: Carbon has 4 valence electrons, allowing it to form 4 stable covalent bonds — with other carbons, H, O, N, and more. This enables the incredible structural diversity of organic molecules: chains, rings, branches.
  • Dehydration synthesis (condensation): Two monomers are joined by removing one H from one monomer and one OH from the other — together forming H₂O. The remaining bond between them is a covalent bond. Repeated to build polymers. Requires energy input.
  • Hydrolysis: The reverse — a water molecule is added across the covalent bond, splitting it into two monomers. H goes to one product, OH to the other. Releases energy. Example: digestion breaks down food polymers into monomers.
Critical MCQ Distinction (Q1): Hydrolysis = ADDITION of water that BREAKS bonds. Dehydration = REMOVAL of water that FORMS bonds. Students frequently mix these up!
Topics 1.4–1.5 — Carbohydrates & Lipids
  • Carbohydrate monomers (monosaccharides): Simple sugars like glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆). The ratio of H:O is always 2:1, same as water. Connected by glycosidic linkages formed by dehydration synthesis.
  • Polysaccharide functions vary with structure: Starch (plant energy storage) and glycogen (animal energy storage) are branched → easy to break down quickly. Cellulose (plant cell wall) has β-glycosidic bonds humans can't digest — gives structural rigidity. Structure determines function!
  • Lipid general properties: Predominantly C–H bonds, which are nonpolar → lipids don't dissolve in water (hydrophobic). This makes them ideal for energy storage (more energy per gram than carbohydrates) and membrane formation.
  • Saturated fats: All C–C single bonds → straight chains that pack tightly together → solid at room temperature (e.g., butter, lard). Associated with animal fats.
  • Unsaturated fats: One or more C=C double bonds → introduces a "kink" in the chain → can't pack as tightly → liquid at room temperature (e.g., olive oil). The more double bonds, the more unsaturated and more fluid.
  • Phospholipid bilayer: Each phospholipid has a hydrophilic phosphate "head" (faces water) and two hydrophobic fatty acid "tails" (face each other, away from water). This self-assembly property is why membranes form spontaneously — following the laws of thermodynamics.
  • Cholesterol: A steroid lipid embedded in animal cell membranes. At moderate temps it reduces fluidity; at low temps it prevents fatty acid tails from packing too tightly, maintaining fluidity. It's a "fluidity buffer."
  • Steroids: Lipids with a 4-ring carbon skeleton. Many hormones are steroids (testosterone, estrogen, cortisol). Being hydrophobic, they can cross the plasma membrane and bind receptors inside the cell, directly influencing gene expression.
NOT Tested: Molecular structure of specific carbohydrate polymers or specific lipids is beyond the scope of the AP Exam.
Topic 1.6 — Nucleic Acids
  • Nucleotide structure (monomer): Each nucleotide = 5-carbon sugar + phosphate group + nitrogenous base. The phosphate and sugar form the "backbone" of the nucleic acid strand via phosphodiester bonds; the bases project inward.
  • DNA vs. RNA sugar: DNA contains deoxyribose (missing an –OH at the 2' carbon), making it more stable. RNA contains ribose (has –OH at 2'). This structural difference is why DNA is preferred for long-term information storage.
  • Base pairing rules (must know): In DNA: Adenine (A) pairs with Thymine (T) via 2 hydrogen bonds; Cytosine (C) pairs with Guanine (G) via 3 hydrogen bonds. In RNA: Uracil (U) replaces Thymine and pairs with Adenine. G-C pairs are stronger than A-T pairs.
  • Purine vs. Pyrimidine: Purines (A, G) = double-ring structure. Pyrimidines (C, T, U) = single-ring. A purine always pairs with a pyrimidine — this keeps the DNA double helix a constant width.
  • Antiparallel orientation: The two DNA strands run in opposite directions. One strand goes 5'→3' while the other goes 3'→5'. The 5' end has a free phosphate group; the 3' end has a free hydroxyl (–OH) group. Nucleotides are always added to the 3' end during replication.
  • DNA double helix: The two antiparallel strands are held together by hydrogen bonds between complementary base pairs, with the hydrophobic bases stacked on top of each other ("base stacking") providing additional stability.
  • Chargaff's rules (tested on exam Q31): In any double-stranded DNA: %A = %T and %G = %C. This is because A always pairs with T and G always pairs with C. Can determine one strand's base composition from the other.
NOT Tested: Molecular structure of specific nucleotides is beyond the scope of the AP Exam.
Topic 1.7 — Proteins
  • Amino acid structure: All 20 amino acids share the same core: central carbon bonded to H, –NH₂ (amine group, makes it basic), –COOH (carboxyl group, makes it acidic), and a unique R group (side chain). The R group determines the amino acid's chemical properties and interactions.
  • Peptide bonds: Form via dehydration synthesis between the carboxyl group (–COOH) of one amino acid and the amine group (–NH₂) of the next, releasing water. The resulting C–N bond is the peptide bond.
  • R group categories: Hydrophobic/nonpolar R groups cause protein regions to fold away from water (toward interior). Hydrophilic/polar R groups face the aqueous environment. Ionic R groups can form ionic interactions. Cysteine has an –SH group that forms disulfide bridges.
  • Primary structure: The unique, specific sequence of amino acids in a polypeptide chain, determined by the gene. Even one amino acid change can dramatically alter protein function (e.g., sickle cell disease from Glu→Val substitution — glutamic acid replaced by valine at position 6 of beta-globin).
  • Secondary structure: Local folding of the polypeptide backbone through hydrogen bonds between the carbonyl oxygen and amino hydrogen of the backbone (not R groups). Forms α-helices (coiled) and β-pleated sheets (folded back).
  • Tertiary structure: The overall 3D shape of the complete polypeptide — determined by interactions between R groups: hydrophobic interactions (R groups cluster away from water), ionic bonds, hydrogen bonds between R groups, and disulfide bridges (–S–S–) between cysteines.
  • Quaternary structure: Exists only when a protein is made of 2+ polypeptide subunits. The subunits associate via the same R-group interactions as tertiary structure. Example: hemoglobin has 4 subunits.
  • Structure determines function: All four structural levels contribute to a protein's shape and therefore function. Denaturation — disruption of 3D shape by heat, pH changes, or chemicals — destroys function even if the amino acid sequence is unchanged.
MCQ Insight (Q39): The R group (not the backbone) determines hydrophobic behavior. A nonpolar R group (like CH₃ — methyl) causes a region to fold away from water. Amine and carboxyl groups are shared by ALL amino acids.
Unit 1 — Common Mistakes & Exam Tips
Protein vs. Proton: The CED explicitly warns students confuse these terms on FRQs — always use precise vocabulary.
R group = variable group: All amino acids share the same backbone; only the R group varies and determines structure/function.
Tip: The structure-function theme is central. Always connect molecular structure to its biological function in explanations.
NOT Tested: Molecular structure of specific amino acids. Specific molecular structures of specific lipids or carbohydrate polymers.
📚 Unit 1 Key Vocabulary
Hydrogen Bond
Weak attraction between δ+ hydrogen and a δ− electronegative atom (O or N). Collectively gives water its unique properties.
Polar Covalent Bond
Bond where electrons are shared unequally, creating partial charges. The O–H bonds in water are polar covalent → water's polarity.
Cohesion
Attraction of water to itself via H-bonds. Causes surface tension; allows water columns to be pulled up through plant xylem.
Adhesion
Attraction of water to other polar surfaces. Combined with cohesion, drives capillary action in plant xylem and paper towels.
Specific Heat Capacity
Energy required to raise 1 g of substance by 1°C. Water's is unusually high — resists temperature swings, stabilizing body temperature and climate.
Heat of Vaporization
Energy needed to convert liquid water to vapor. High value = evaporation removes large amounts of heat → basis of sweating and evaporative cooling.
Hydrophilic
Water-loving; dissolves in or interacts favorably with water. Includes polar molecules, ions, and sugars.
Hydrophobic
Water-fearing; excluded by water. Nonpolar molecules like oils and fats. Drives phospholipid bilayer formation and protein folding.
Monomer
Small repeating subunit that links to form polymers via dehydration synthesis. Examples: glucose (carb), amino acid (protein), nucleotide (DNA/RNA).
Polymer
Large macromolecule built from many monomers. Examples: starch, proteins, DNA. Broken apart by hydrolysis.
Dehydration Synthesis
Reaction that builds polymers by removing H₂O from joining monomers. Also called condensation reaction.
Hydrolysis
Reaction that breaks polymers by adding H₂O across the covalent bond. Used in digestion. Hydrolysis ≠ dehydration!
Monosaccharide
Simple sugar; monomer of carbohydrates. Example: glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆). H:O ratio is always 2:1.
Polysaccharide
Complex carbohydrate polymer. Starch/glycogen = energy storage. Cellulose = structural support. Structure dictates function.
Saturated Fatty Acid
Fatty acid with only single C–C bonds; chains pack tightly → solid at room temperature. Found in animal fats (butter, lard).
Unsaturated Fatty Acid
Fatty acid with ≥1 C=C double bond; creates a kink, preventing tight packing → liquid at room temperature (oils). More double bonds = more unsaturated.
Phospholipid
Amphipathic lipid with a hydrophilic phosphate head and two hydrophobic fatty acid tails. Self-assembles into bilayers — the basis of all cell membranes.
Steroid
Lipid with a 4-ring carbon skeleton. Examples: cholesterol (membrane fluidity buffer), testosterone, estrogen (hormones that diffuse into cells and alter gene expression).
Nucleotide
Monomer of nucleic acids: 5-carbon sugar + phosphate group + nitrogenous base. Linked by phosphodiester bonds to form DNA/RNA strands.
Purine
Double-ring nitrogenous base: Adenine (A) and Guanine (G). Always pairs with a pyrimidine. Purines are larger than pyrimidines.
Pyrimidine
Single-ring nitrogenous base: Cytosine (C), Thymine (T — DNA only), Uracil (U — RNA only). Always pairs with a purine.
Amino Acid
Monomer of proteins; has central C bonded to: H, –NH₂ (amine), –COOH (carboxyl), and a unique R group determining chemical properties.
Peptide Bond
Covalent bond between –COOH of one amino acid and –NH₂ of the next; formed by dehydration synthesis. Links amino acids in a chain.
Primary Structure
The specific sequence of amino acids in a polypeptide. Determined by DNA. Foundation for all higher levels of protein structure.
Secondary Structure
Local folding via H-bonds between backbone atoms (not R groups). Creates α-helices (coils) and β-pleated sheets (zigzag). R groups not involved.
Tertiary Structure
Overall 3D shape from R-group interactions: hydrophobic interactions, ionic bonds, H-bonds between R groups, disulfide bridges (–S–S–).
Quaternary Structure
Two or more polypeptide chains associate into one functional protein. Not all proteins have this level. Example: hemoglobin (4 subunits).
Denaturation
Disruption of protein 3D structure by heat, extreme pH, or chemicals — breaks non-covalent interactions. Destroys function. May or may not be reversible.

Unit 2 — Cells

The cell is the basic unit of life. This unit covers organelle structure/function, membrane biology, transport mechanisms, compartmentalization, and the origin of eukaryotic cells via endosymbiosis.

Topic 2.1 — Cell Structure & Function
  • Ribosomes: Made of rRNA + protein; synthesize proteins from mRNA; found in ALL forms of life → evidence of common ancestry
  • Endomembrane system: ER, Golgi, lysosomes, vacuoles, vesicles, nuclear envelope, plasma membrane — work together to modify, package, and transport molecules
  • Rough ER: Membrane-bound ribosomes → protein synthesis; compartmentalization
  • Smooth ER: Detoxification + lipid synthesis
  • Golgi complex: Correctly folds, chemically modifies, and packages proteins for trafficking
  • Mitochondria: Double membrane; inner membrane folded (cristae → more surface area for ATP synthesis); site of aerobic respiration
  • Lysosomes: Contain hydrolytic enzymes; digest material; role in apoptosis
  • Vacuoles: Plant = large central vacuole (turgor pressure); animal = smaller, more numerous
  • Chloroplasts: Plants & photosynthetic algae; double membrane; site of photosynthesis
Common Error: Students correctly name organelles but fail to describe their function with precision. Know structure → function connections at the subcellular AND cellular level.
Topic 2.2 — Cell Size & SA:V Ratio
  • SA:V ratio affects ability to exchange materials, nutrients, waste, and thermal energy
  • As cell volume ↑, SA:V ratio ↓ → less efficient exchange
  • Smaller cells = higher SA:V = more efficient
  • As organism size ↑, SA:V ↓ → rate of heat exchange ↓ (larger animals lose less heat proportionally)
  • Smaller organisms have higher metabolic rate per unit body mass
Key Equations — Cell Geometry
SA:V Sphere = (4πr²) / (4/3 πr³)
Also know cube, rectangular solid, cylinder formulas. SA:V ratio is the key metric.
MCQ Insight (Q18): Larger grapes absorb water MORE SLOWLY per gram because they have a SMALLER SA:V ratio. Rate of absorption scales with surface area, not volume.
Topics 2.3–2.4 — Plasma Membrane
  • Fluid mosaic model: Phospholipid bilayer with embedded proteins, cholesterol, glycoproteins, glycolipids — all can move laterally
  • Phospholipids: Hydrophilic head (phosphate, toward water) + hydrophobic tail (fatty acids, face inward)
  • Selective permeability: Due to hydrophobic interior of membrane
  • Freely cross: Small nonpolar molecules (N₂, O₂, CO₂); small uncharged polar (H₂O, NH₃) in small amounts
  • Need protein channels: Large polar molecules, ions
  • Cell walls: Bacteria, Archaea, Fungi, plants — provide structural boundary, permeability barrier, protection from osmotic lysis
NOT Tested: Specific functions of smooth ER in specialized cells.
Topics 2.5–2.8 — Transport Mechanisms
  • Passive transport: High → low concentration; no energy required; includes simple diffusion + facilitated diffusion
  • Active transport: Requires energy (ATP); can move against concentration gradient; requires membrane proteins
  • Na⁺/K⁺ pump (ATPase): Maintains membrane potential via active transport
  • Facilitated diffusion: Uses channel or carrier proteins; no energy; moves down concentration gradient
  • Aquaporins: Channel proteins for rapid water transport across membrane (facilitated diffusion of water)
  • Endocytosis: Cell takes in large material by membrane folding inward → vesicle forms (requires energy)
  • Exocytosis: Vesicle fuses with plasma membrane → secretes large molecules out (requires energy)
MCQ Insight (Q17): Inhibiting aquaporins slows water entry (it becomes slower facilitated diffusion, not zero — water can still move by simple diffusion, just slower). Active transport is NOT involved in osmosis.
Topic 2.7 — Tonicity & Water Potential
  • Osmosis: Net movement of water from high water potential → low water potential (hypotonic → hypertonic)
  • Hypertonic solution: More solutes than cell → water leaves cell → cell shrinks (plasmolysis in plants)
  • Hypotonic solution: Fewer solutes than cell → water enters cell → cell swells
  • Isotonic: Equal solute concentration → no net water movement
Water Potential (Ψ)
Ψ = Ψₚ + Ψₛ
Ψₚ = pressure potential | Ψₛ = solute potential
Solute potential: Ψₛ = −iCRT
i = ionization constant | C = molar concentration
R = 0.0831 L·bar/mol·K | T = temperature (Kelvin)
MCQ Insight (Q13–16): Water moves INTO the solution with LOWER water potential. If grape mass decreases, the solution is HYPERTONIC to the grape (lower water potential). Solute concentration of the grape ≈ the concentration where percent change ≈ 0.
Topics 2.9–2.10 — Compartmentalization & Endosymbiosis
  • Compartmentalization: Internal membranes minimize competing reactions; increase surface area for reactions
  • Prokaryotes: Lack membrane-bound organelles; have internal regions with specialized structure
  • Eukaryotes: Membrane-bound organelles partition cell into specialized regions
  • Endosymbiosis: Mitochondria and chloroplasts evolved from once free-living prokaryotes engulfed by ancestral cells
MCQ Insight (Q56): Strongest evidence for endosymbiosis = mitochondria have their OWN DNA and divide independently of the cell. This mirrors free-living bacteria. Folded membranes are structural, not evolutionary evidence.
📚 Unit 2 Key Vocabulary
Prokaryote
Cell lacking a membrane-bound nucleus and membrane-bound organelles. DNA is in a nucleoid region. Includes Bacteria and Archaea. Typically smaller than eukaryotes.
Eukaryote
Cell with a membrane-bound nucleus and membrane-bound organelles. Includes plants, animals, fungi, and protists. Arose via endosymbiosis.
Ribosome
Non-membrane organelle made of rRNA + protein; synthesizes proteins. Found in ALL cells — evidence of common ancestry. Prokaryotes have 70S; eukaryotes have 80S ribosomes.
Endomembrane System
Interconnected membrane organelles working together to synthesize, modify, and transport proteins and lipids: ER, Golgi, lysosomes, vacuoles, vesicles, nuclear envelope, plasma membrane.
Rough ER
Endoplasmic reticulum studded with ribosomes. Site of synthesis of membrane proteins and secreted proteins. Proteins enter the ER lumen for folding and modification.
Smooth ER
ER without ribosomes. Functions include lipid synthesis, detoxification of drugs/poisons (e.g., in liver cells), and calcium storage (in muscle cells).
Golgi Complex
"Post office" of the cell. Receives vesicles from the ER, further modifies proteins, sorts them, and packages them into vesicles for delivery to destinations inside or outside the cell.
Lysosome
Membrane-enclosed sac containing hydrolytic (digestive) enzymes. Digests food particles, worn-out organelles (autophagy), and bacteria. Also triggers apoptosis (programmed cell death).
Mitochondria
Double-membraned organelle; site of aerobic cellular respiration and ATP production. Inner membrane (cristae) is highly folded to increase surface area. Has own DNA — evidence of endosymbiosis.
Chloroplast
Double-membraned organelle in plants and algae; site of photosynthesis. Contains thylakoids (site of light reactions) and stroma (site of Calvin cycle). Has own DNA — evidence of endosymbiosis.
Fluid Mosaic Model
Description of the plasma membrane as a fluid phospholipid bilayer with proteins, cholesterol, glycoproteins, and glycolipids embedded within it — all able to move laterally.
Selective Permeability
Property of membranes allowing some substances to cross freely while restricting others, based on size, polarity, and charge. Results from the hydrophobic interior of the bilayer.
Diffusion
Net movement of molecules from high to low concentration (down the gradient) — no energy required. Small nonpolar molecules (O₂, CO₂) diffuse through the lipid bilayer freely.
Osmosis
Diffusion of water specifically, through a selectively permeable membrane. Water moves from high water potential (low solute) to low water potential (high solute).
Facilitated Diffusion
Passive transport of large polar molecules or ions through specific channel or carrier proteins. Moves down concentration gradient; no energy required. Example: glucose transporters, aquaporins.
Active Transport
Movement of molecules against their concentration gradient using energy (ATP) and membrane protein pumps. Example: Na⁺/K⁺ pump — moves 3 Na⁺ out and 2 K⁺ in per ATP.
Aquaporin
Channel protein specialized for rapid water transport across the membrane. Allows much faster osmosis than simple diffusion through the lipid bilayer. Inhibited by mercurial compounds.
Endocytosis
Cell takes in large molecules or particles by folding the plasma membrane inward to form a new vesicle. Requires energy. Types: phagocytosis (large solids), pinocytosis (fluids), receptor-mediated.
Exocytosis
Vesicles inside the cell fuse with the plasma membrane and release their contents outside the cell. Requires energy. Used for secretion of proteins, neurotransmitters, hormones.
Tonicity
Relative solute concentration of a solution compared to the cell's interior. Determines direction of net water movement across the membrane.
Hypertonic
Solution with higher solute concentration than the cell. Water leaves the cell → cell shrinks (crenation in animal cells, plasmolysis in plant cells).
Hypotonic
Solution with lower solute concentration than the cell. Water enters the cell → cell swells; plant cells become turgid (firm), animal cells may lyse (burst).
Isotonic
Solution with equal solute concentration to the cell. No net water movement. Animal cells are ideal; plant cells lack turgor pressure and go limp.
Water Potential (Ψ)
Measure of free energy of water; determines direction of water movement. Ψ = Ψₚ (pressure potential) + Ψₛ (solute potential). Water moves from high Ψ to low Ψ.
Surface Area-to-Volume Ratio
As cell size increases, SA:V ratio decreases — limiting efficiency of material exchange. Smaller cells have higher SA:V ratios and more efficient transport.
Endosymbiosis
Theory that mitochondria and chloroplasts evolved from free-living prokaryotes engulfed by ancestral eukaryotes. Evidence: they have their own circular DNA and divide by binary fission.

Unit 3 — Cellular Energetics

Covers enzyme function, photosynthesis, and cellular respiration. One of the highest-weighted units (12–16%). Focus on inputs, outputs, and locations — not memorization of specific steps or enzyme names.

Topics 3.1–3.2 — Enzymes
  • Enzymes: Biological catalysts (proteins) that lower activation energy; not consumed in reactions
  • Active site: Shape + charge must be compatible with substrate (enzyme-substrate complex)
  • Temperature: Higher temp → more collisions → faster rate — until optimal; beyond optimal → denaturation (H-bonds disrupted)
  • pH: Outside optimal → changes structure → alters efficiency
  • Denaturation: Some is reversible
  • Substrate concentration: Higher substrate → faster rate (until enzyme saturated)
  • Competitive inhibition: Inhibitor binds reversibly to active site; can be overcome by adding more substrate
  • Noncompetitive (allosteric) inhibition: Binds allosteric site → changes active site shape; increasing substrate CANNOT overcome it
MCQ Insight (Q21): Alanine inhibits pyruvate kinase and cannot be overcome by increasing substrate → allosteric inhibition (binds a different site, changes active site shape). Competitive inhibition CAN be overcome by more substrate.
Topic 3.3 — Cellular Energy & Thermodynamics
  • All living systems require energy input to maintain order
  • Life does NOT violate 1st or 2nd laws of thermodynamics — energy input must exceed energy loss to maintain order
  • Coupled reactions: Exergonic (energy-releasing) reactions can be coupled to power endergonic (energy-requiring) reactions
  • ATP hydrolysis: Exergonic — releases energy to power endergonic processes (like phosphorylation)
  • Metabolic pathways: Sequential — product of one step is reactant for next
  • Core pathways (glycolysis, oxidative phosphorylation) are conserved across all domains of life → evidence of common ancestry
MCQ Insight (Q47): ATP hydrolysis (exergonic = energy released = line goes downhill from reactants) is coupled with phosphorylation (endergonic = energy required = line goes uphill). The exergonic reaction releases energy to drive the endergonic one.
NOT Tested: Gibbs free energy equation. Specific enzyme names in glycolysis or Krebs cycle.
Topic 3.4 — Photosynthesis
  • Overall: CO₂ + H₂O + light energy → carbohydrates + O₂
  • Chloroplast structure: Stroma (fluid) + thylakoids (membranes in stacks called grana)
  • Light reactions (grana/thylakoids): Chlorophyll captures light; water splits (O₂ released); electrons energized through Photosystems I and II; ATP and NADPH produced via chemiosmosis (photophosphorylation)
  • Calvin cycle (stroma): Uses ATP + NADPH from light reactions; CO₂ fixed into carbohydrates
  • ETC in photosynthesis: Electrons pass through thylakoid membrane; proton gradient formed; ATP synthase uses proton flow (chemiosmosis) to make ATP
  • First evolved in prokaryotes (cyanobacteria) → responsible for oxygenated atmosphere
MCQ Insight (Q41): If thylakoid membranes become permeable to H⁺ → proton gradient collapses → ATP synthase cannot function → LESS ATP produced. This directly disrupts chemiosmosis/photophosphorylation.
NOT Tested: Steps of Calvin cycle, names of molecules, specific enzyme names except ATP synthase.
Topic 3.5 — Cellular Respiration
  • Glycolysis (cytosol): Glucose → 2 pyruvate; produces ATP and NADH; occurs in ALL cells
  • Pyruvate oxidation: Pyruvate transported to mitochondrial matrix → CO₂ released; NADH produced
  • Krebs cycle (mitochondrial matrix): CO₂ released; ATP, NADH, FADH₂ produced
  • Electron transport chain (inner mitochondrial membrane): NADH and FADH₂ deliver electrons; electrons pass to O₂ (terminal acceptor); proton gradient formed across inner membrane
  • Oxidative phosphorylation: H⁺ flow through ATP synthase → ATP; inner membrane folding (cristae) ↑ surface area → more ATP
  • Fermentation: Anaerobic; allows glycolysis to continue; products = alcohol or lactic acid
MCQ Insight (Q4): Bacteria producing lactic acid = fermentation = anaerobic process. They do NOT have mitochondria. Lactic acid fermentation regenerates NAD⁺ to keep glycolysis going without O₂.

📚 Unit 3 Key Vocabulary
Enzyme
Biological catalyst (protein) that speeds up chemical reactions by lowering activation energy. Not consumed in the reaction; can be reused. Highly specific to their substrates.
Substrate
The specific reactant molecule that an enzyme acts on. Binds to the enzyme's active site. Complementary in shape and charge to the active site.
Active Site
The specific region of an enzyme where the substrate binds. Has a unique shape and chemical environment. Changes in temperature or pH can alter active site shape.
Activation Energy
Energy required to initiate a chemical reaction. Enzymes lower activation energy by stabilizing the transition state, allowing reactions to proceed faster at physiological temperatures.
Enzyme-Substrate Complex
The temporary association of enzyme and substrate during catalysis. Shape and charge of substrate must be complementary to the active site for the complex to form.
Competitive Inhibitor
Molecule structurally similar to the substrate that binds to the active site and blocks substrate access. Can be overcome by increasing substrate concentration.
Noncompetitive (Allosteric) Inhibitor
Binds to an allosteric site (not the active site), changing the enzyme's shape and reducing catalytic activity. Cannot be overcome by increasing substrate concentration.
Allosteric Site
A regulatory site on an enzyme distinct from the active site. Binding of molecules here changes the enzyme's shape — either activating or inhibiting it.
Denaturation
Loss of enzyme's 3D shape due to extreme temperature, pH, or chemicals — disrupts H-bonds and other weak interactions. Eliminates catalytic ability. May be reversible or irreversible.
Photosynthesis
Process by which photosynthetic organisms convert light energy into chemical energy (glucose). Overall: CO₂ + H₂O + light → C₆H₁₂O₆ + O₂. Occurs in chloroplasts.
Chlorophyll
Green pigment in thylakoid membranes that absorbs light energy (primarily red and blue wavelengths; reflects green). Two types organized into Photosystems I and II.
Light Reactions
First stage of photosynthesis; occurs in the thylakoid membranes. Uses light to split water (releasing O₂), energize electrons, and produce ATP and NADPH via the ETC and chemiosmosis.
Calvin Cycle
Second stage of photosynthesis; occurs in the stroma. Uses ATP and NADPH from light reactions to fix CO₂ into organic molecules (sugars). Also called carbon fixation or C3 cycle.
Thylakoid
Flattened membrane sacs inside the chloroplast stacked into grana. Site of the light reactions. Contain chlorophyll and the electron transport chain.
Stroma
Fluid-filled space inside the inner chloroplast membrane and surrounding the thylakoids. Site of the Calvin cycle (carbon fixation reactions).
Chemiosmosis
ATP synthesis driven by the flow of H⁺ ions (protons) through ATP synthase down an electrochemical gradient. Occurs in both mitochondria (oxidative phosphorylation) and chloroplasts (photophosphorylation).
ATP Synthase
Enzyme embedded in membrane; uses the flow of H⁺ ions through it (chemiosmosis) to phosphorylate ADP → ATP. Found in thylakoid and inner mitochondrial membranes.
Cellular Respiration
Process of breaking down organic molecules (glucose) to produce ATP. Overall: C₆H₁₂O₆ + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O + ATP. Includes glycolysis, Krebs cycle, and ETC.
Glycolysis
First stage of cellular respiration; occurs in cytosol. Breaks glucose (6C) into 2 pyruvate (3C) molecules. Produces 2 ATP and 2 NADH. Occurs in ALL cells; does not require oxygen.
Krebs Cycle (Citric Acid Cycle)
Second stage of aerobic respiration; occurs in mitochondrial matrix. Oxidizes pyruvate-derived acetyl-CoA; releases CO₂, and produces NADH, FADH₂, and ATP per turn.
Electron Transport Chain (ETC)
Series of membrane-bound protein complexes that pass electrons from NADH/FADH₂ to O₂ (terminal electron acceptor in aerobic respiration). Creates the proton gradient used for ATP synthesis.
NADH / FADH₂
Electron carriers produced in glycolysis and the Krebs cycle. Donate electrons to the ETC, releasing energy used to pump H⁺ ions and drive ATP synthesis via chemiosmosis.
Oxidative Phosphorylation
ATP production in aerobic respiration via chemiosmosis — H⁺ ions flow through ATP synthase in the inner mitochondrial membrane. Accounts for the majority of ATP produced.
Fermentation
Anaerobic process that regenerates NAD⁺ from NADH so glycolysis can continue. Produces lactic acid (in animals/bacteria) or ethanol + CO₂ (in yeast). Net yield: 2 ATP per glucose.
Photophosphorylation
ATP synthesis in chloroplasts via chemiosmosis during the light reactions. H⁺ gradient builds across the thylakoid membrane; H⁺ flows through ATP synthase to make ATP.
NADPH
Electron carrier made in the light reactions of photosynthesis. Carries high-energy electrons to the Calvin cycle to power CO₂ fixation into sugar.

Unit 4 — Cell Communication & Cell Cycle

How cells communicate over short and long distances, signal transduction pathways, feedback mechanisms, and how the cell cycle is regulated. Expect scenario-based MCQs applying signaling principles to drugs or diseases.

Topics 4.1–4.2 — Cell Communication
  • Direct contact: Cells physically touch to communicate
  • Local regulators: Signal molecules target nearby cells (short distance)
  • Hormones: Travel long distances through bloodstream to target cells (e.g., insulin, testosterone, estrogen)
  • Signal transduction: Ligand binds receptor → receptor changes shape → cascade initiated → cellular response
  • Ligand: Chemical messenger (peptide/small molecule) that binds receptor's ligand-binding domain
  • Receptor location: Surface of cell, cytoplasm, or nucleus depending on ligand type
  • G protein-coupled receptors: Common receptor type in eukaryotes
  • Phosphorylation cascades: Kinases activate other proteins; signal is amplified at each step
  • cAMP: Second messenger that relays and amplifies intracellular signal
MCQ Insight (Q12): Thickening arrows in a cascade = SIGNAL AMPLIFICATION. A few initial signaling molecules activate MANY molecules at each step, producing a large cellular response.
Topics 4.3–4.4 — Signaling & Feedback
  • Signal transduction responses: Can alter gene expression, cell function, phenotype, or trigger apoptosis (programmed cell death)
  • Mutations in signaling: Any mutation in receptor or pathway component → altered downstream response; chemicals can activate OR inhibit pathway
  • Negative feedback: Response reduces original stimulus → returns system to set point (homeostasis). Example: blood glucose regulation by insulin
  • Positive feedback: Response amplifies original stimulus; moves further from set point. Example: oxytocin during childbirth, ethylene during fruit ripening
MCQ Insight (Q33): Ethylene ripening = POSITIVE feedback: ripening fruit produces ethylene → stimulates other cells to produce more ethylene → accelerates ripening. The key indicator of positive feedback is amplification/acceleration of the original process.
MCQ Insight (Q29): In the insulin-osteocalcin pathway: insulin → positive feedback loop → more insulin secreted → low blood sugar in mutant mice. Recognize positive feedback loops by the escalating output.
Topic 4.5 — The Cell Cycle
  • Interphase stages:
    • • G₁: Cell grows; duplicates organelles and cytosolic components
    • • S phase: DNA replicates → sister chromatids form, connected at centromere
    • • G₂: Further cell growth; protein synthesis; preparation for division; centrosome duplication completes (began in S phase)
  • Mitosis stages: Prophase → Metaphase → Anaphase → Telophase
  • Prophase: Chromatids condense; spindle forms; centrosomes move to poles
  • Metaphase: Chromosomes align at equator (metaphase plate)
  • Anaphase: Sister chromatids separate; pulled to opposite poles
  • Telophase: Nuclear envelope reforms; spindle breaks down
  • Cytokinesis: Cleavage furrow (animal) or cell plate (plant) → 2 genetically identical daughter cells
  • G₀: Non-dividing state; cells can re-enter cycle with appropriate signals
Topic 4.6 — Cell Cycle Regulation
  • Checkpoints: Internal controls that regulate progression through the cell cycle
  • Cyclins + cyclin-dependent kinases (CdKs): Interactions control the cell cycle
  • Cancer: Results from disruptions to cell cycle regulation — uncontrolled cell division
  • Apoptosis: Programmed cell death — can result from cell cycle disruption
Common Error: Students don't explain the purpose and timing of checkpoints. Know: G₁ checkpoint (commit to divide?), G₂ checkpoint (DNA replicated correctly?), and metaphase checkpoint (chromosomes attached to spindle?).
NOT Tested: Specific cyclin-CdK pairs or specific growth factor names.
Exam Prep: Be ready to compare mitosis and meiosis — this is a frequent FRQ and MCQ topic.
📚 Unit 4 Key Vocabulary
Ligand
A chemical signaling molecule (e.g., hormone, neurotransmitter) that binds specifically to a complementary receptor protein to initiate signal transduction. "Signal" in cell communication.
Receptor Protein
Cell surface or intracellular protein that specifically binds a ligand. Binding causes a conformational (shape) change that initiates an intracellular response cascade.
Signal Transduction
The process by which a cell converts an extracellular signal (ligand binding) into an intracellular response. Involves a cascade of molecular changes within the cell.
G Protein-Coupled Receptor (GPCR)
Common type of cell surface receptor in eukaryotes. When activated by ligand, activates an associated G protein that relays the signal to downstream effectors inside the cell.
Phosphorylation Cascade
Series of protein activations where each kinase (enzyme) phosphorylates and activates the next protein in the chain. Amplifies the signal dramatically at each step.
Second Messenger
Small intracellular molecules that relay and amplify signals from receptors to target molecules inside the cell. Example: cAMP (cyclic AMP) — activated by adenylyl cyclase in response to receptor activation.
Signal Amplification
Each step of a signaling cascade activates many molecules at the next step, so a few initial signals result in a massive cellular response. Represented by widening arrows in cascade diagrams.
Negative Feedback
The response reduces the original stimulus, returning the system to its set point. Maintains homeostasis. Example: rising blood glucose → insulin released → glucose uptake → blood glucose falls.
Positive Feedback
The response amplifies the original stimulus, moving the system further from the set point. Example: oxytocin during childbirth; ethylene in fruit ripening — the response produces more of the initiating signal.
Homeostasis
Maintenance of a stable internal environment despite external changes. Maintained primarily by negative feedback loops operating at molecular, cellular, and organismal levels.
Apoptosis
Programmed cell death — an orderly, genetically controlled process of cell self-destruction. Important for development, immune function, and removing damaged cells. Triggered by signals or DNA damage.
Cell Cycle
Ordered sequence of events by which a cell duplicates its contents and divides into two daughter cells. Consists of interphase (G₁, S, G₂) and the mitotic phase (mitosis + cytokinesis).
Interphase
The period between cell divisions; comprises ~90% of the cell cycle. G₁ (cell growth), S phase (DNA replication), and G₂ (preparation for division). Cell actively metabolizes throughout.
S Phase
"Synthesis" phase of interphase. DNA replicates so each chromosome is duplicated into two identical sister chromatids joined at the centromere. Centrosomes also duplicate.
Sister Chromatids
The two identical copies of a replicated chromosome held together at the centromere after DNA replication during S phase. Separated during anaphase of mitosis (or anaphase II of meiosis).
Mitosis
Division of the nucleus into two genetically identical daughter nuclei. Phases: Prophase, Metaphase, Anaphase, Telophase (PMAT). Followed by cytokinesis. Used for growth and repair.
Cytokinesis
Division of the cytoplasm following mitosis. In animal cells: cleavage furrow forms. In plant cells: cell plate forms. Produces two complete daughter cells.
Spindle Apparatus
Microtubule structure that forms during mitosis/meiosis. Attaches to chromosomes at the centromere via kinetochores and moves chromosomes to opposite poles of the cell.
Checkpoint
Control point in the cell cycle where the cell "checks" for errors before proceeding. G₁ checkpoint: ready to divide? G₂ checkpoint: DNA replicated correctly? Metaphase checkpoint: chromosomes attached to spindle?
Cyclin
Regulatory protein whose concentration fluctuates cyclically during the cell cycle. When cyclin binds to a Cdk (cyclin-dependent kinase), it activates the kinase to phosphorylate target proteins and advance the cell cycle.
Cancer
Disease of uncontrolled cell division resulting from mutations in genes that regulate the cell cycle (proto-oncogenes, tumor suppressor genes). Cells ignore growth signals and checkpoints.
G₀ Phase
Non-dividing state where cells exit the cell cycle. Mature neurons and muscle cells are often in G₀. Cells can re-enter the cycle in response to growth signals.

Unit 5 — Heredity

Meiosis, Mendelian genetics, non-Mendelian inheritance, chi-square analysis, and environmental effects on phenotype. Students must be able to calculate genotypic and phenotypic ratios and interpret pedigrees.

Topics 5.1–5.2 — Meiosis
  • Purpose: Produces haploid gametes (n) from diploid (2n) cells
  • Meiosis I (reductive division): Separates homologous chromosome pairs → 2 haploid cells
  • Prophase I: Homologs pair (synapsis); chiasmata form; crossing over (recombination) occurs → increases genetic diversity
  • Metaphase I: Homologous pairs align at metaphase plate (independent assortment)
  • Anaphase I: Homologs separate; sister chromatids remain attached
  • Meiosis II: Sister chromatids separate → 4 haploid daughter cells
  • Sources of genetic variation: Crossing over, independent assortment of chromosomes, fertilization
  • Nondisjunction: Incorrect separation → aneuploid gametes (not haploid)
Mitosis vs. Meiosis: Both use spindle apparatus. Mitosis → 2 genetically identical diploid cells (growth/repair). Meiosis → 4 genetically unique haploid cells (reproduction).
Topic 5.3 — Mendelian Genetics
  • Law of Segregation: Two alleles for a gene separate during gamete formation
  • Law of Independent Assortment: Genes on different chromosomes assort independently
  • Monohybrid cross: 3:1 phenotype ratio (dominant:recessive) when both parents heterozygous
  • Dihybrid cross: 9:3:3:1 phenotype ratio when both traits dominant and genes on different chromosomes
  • Test cross: Unknown × homozygous recessive → determines genotype
  • Pedigree analysis: Autosomal dominant: affected parent always passes trait; autosomal recessive: two unaffected carriers can have affected child
Probability Rules
P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) [mutually exclusive]
P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B)
MCQ Insight (Q3): Autosomal recessive: two UNAFFECTED parents (I-1 and I-2) produce an AFFECTED offspring → both parents must be carriers. Sex-linked recessive would show affected females only if father is affected, not the case here.
MCQ Insight (Q30): In a dihybrid cross of double heterozygotes, 9/16 are expected to show both dominant traits. Total offspring × 9/16 = expected number with both traits.
Topic 5.4 — Non-Mendelian Genetics
  • Genetic linkage: Genes on same chromosome; don't assort independently; map distance calculated from recombination frequency
  • Codominance: Both alleles fully expressed; heterozygote shows BOTH phenotypes (not a blend)
  • Incomplete dominance: Neither allele masks the other; heterozygote = blended phenotype (e.g., red × white = pink)
  • Sex-linked traits (X-linked): Genes on sex chromosomes; higher rates in XY individuals; inheritance follows pedigree patterns
  • Pleiotropy: One gene → multiple traits; traits don't segregate independently
  • Non-nuclear inheritance: Chloroplast and mitochondrial DNA; maternally inherited (transmitted via egg/ovule, not sperm/pollen)
Topic 5.5 & Chi-Square Analysis
  • Environmental effects on phenotype: Same genotype can produce different phenotypes under different conditions → phenotypic plasticity (e.g., arctic fox fur color by season)
  • Chi-square test: Compares OBSERVED vs. EXPECTED results to evaluate null hypothesis
  • Null hypothesis: States the independent variable has NO EFFECT on dependent variable
  • Reject null: χ² calculated > χ² critical (at given p-value and degrees of freedom)
  • Fail to reject null: χ² calculated < χ² critical
Chi-Square Formula
χ² = Σ [(O − E)² / E]
O = Observed | E = Expected | Degrees of freedom = (categories − 1)
MCQ Insight (Q40): Two degrees of freedom, p = 0.05: critical value = 5.99. Calculated value = 7.3 > 5.99 → REJECT null hypothesis. The critical value is LESS than the calculated value.
MCQ Insight (Q44): Arctic fox fur color change by season = environmental influence on gene EXPRESSION (phenotypic plasticity). The DNA doesn't mutate — gene expression changes with environmental cues like temperature/day length.
📚 Unit 5 Key Vocabulary
Meiosis
Cell division that produces 4 haploid (n) daughter cells from 1 diploid (2n) parent cell. Occurs in two divisions (Meiosis I and II). Generates genetic diversity through crossing over and independent assortment.
Homologous Chromosomes
A pair of chromosomes — one maternal, one paternal — that carry genes for the same traits at the same loci. Pair during Prophase I of meiosis (synapsis). Not the same as sister chromatids.
Crossing Over (Recombination)
Exchange of DNA segments between non-sister chromatids of homologous chromosome pairs during Prophase I. Creates new combinations of alleles (recombinant chromosomes), increasing genetic diversity.
Chiasma (pl. chiasmata)
The X-shaped physical connection point where crossing over occurs between homologous chromosomes. Visible during Prophase I of meiosis under a microscope.
Independent Assortment
During Metaphase I, homologous pairs orient randomly at the metaphase plate — maternal and paternal chromosomes can end up on either side. Creates 2ⁿ possible gamete combinations (n = haploid chromosome number).
Nondisjunction
Failure of chromosomes to separate properly during meiosis I or II. Results in gametes with abnormal chromosome numbers (aneuploidy). Can cause conditions like trisomy 21 (Down syndrome).
Haploid (n)
Having one complete set of chromosomes. Human gametes are haploid (n = 23). Produced by meiosis. Fertilization restores the diploid number.
Diploid (2n)
Having two complete sets of chromosomes — one from each parent. Human somatic cells are diploid (2n = 46). Produced by fertilization and maintained through mitosis.
Allele
A specific version of a gene at a particular locus. Organisms typically have two alleles per gene (one on each homolog). Can be dominant, recessive, or codominant relative to each other.
Locus (pl. loci)
The specific physical location (position) of a gene on a chromosome. Alleles of the same gene are found at the same locus on homologous chromosomes.
Genotype
The specific combination of alleles an organism has for a trait (e.g., AA, Aa, aa). Determines potential phenotype but can be modified by environment and other genes.
Phenotype
The observable trait or characteristic expressed by an organism. Results from the genotype interacting with the environment. Same genotype can produce different phenotypes under different conditions.
Dominant Allele
An allele whose phenotype is expressed whenever it is present, whether homozygous (AA) or heterozygous (Aa). Masks the effect of the recessive allele.
Recessive Allele
An allele whose phenotype is only expressed when two copies are present (aa — homozygous recessive). Masked by the dominant allele when heterozygous.
Codominance
Both alleles are fully expressed in the heterozygote, producing a distinct phenotype from either homozygote. Example: AB blood type; roan coat color in cattle.
Incomplete Dominance
Neither allele fully masks the other; the heterozygote shows a blended phenotype. Example: red flower × white flower → pink flowers (Rr).
Sex-Linked (X-Linked) Trait
Trait controlled by a gene on the X chromosome. Expressed more often in XY (males) because they have only one X allele. Examples: red-green color blindness, hemophilia.
Pleiotropy
One gene affects multiple traits. The traits do not segregate independently because they are all controlled by the same gene. Example: the CFTR gene mutation in cystic fibrosis affects many organ systems.
Genetic Linkage
Genes located on the same chromosome tend to be inherited together (don't sort independently). Recombination frequency between linked genes can be used to calculate map distance.
Pedigree
Chart showing inheritance patterns of a trait through multiple generations. Used to determine if a trait is autosomal/sex-linked, dominant/recessive, and to determine carrier status.
Dihybrid Cross
Cross between individuals heterozygous for two different genes (AaBb × AaBb). When genes are on different chromosomes, the expected phenotypic ratio is 9:3:3:1.
Chi-Square (χ²) Test
Statistical test comparing observed vs. expected results to evaluate whether deviations from expected ratios are due to chance. If χ² calculated > critical value, reject the null hypothesis.
Phenotypic Plasticity
Ability of a single genotype to produce different phenotypes under different environmental conditions. Example: arctic fox — dark fur in summer, white in winter, same genome.
Maternal Inheritance
Inheritance pattern of mitochondrial and chloroplast genes — transmitted through the egg (not sperm). These organelles have their own DNA not subject to Mendelian rules.

Unit 6 — Gene Expression & Regulation

The most conceptually dense unit (12–16%). Covers DNA replication, transcription, translation, gene regulation, mutations, and biotechnology. The central dogma — DNA → RNA → Protein — is the organizing framework.

Topics 6.1–6.2 — DNA Structure & Replication
  • Prokaryotes: Circular chromosomes (typically); may also have plasmids
  • Eukaryotes: Multiple linear chromosomes; DNA wound around histones; organized into chromatin within the nucleus
  • Purines (double ring): Adenine (A), Guanine (G)
  • Pyrimidines (single ring): Cytosine (C), Thymine (T), Uracil (U)
  • Pairing: A-T (DNA) / A-U (RNA); C-G (always)
  • Replication is semiconservative: Each new double helix has one parental + one new strand
  • Direction: DNA synthesized 5'→3'; template read 3'→5'
  • Key enzymes: Helicase (unwinds), Topoisomerase (relieves supercoiling), Primase (makes short RNA primers), DNA polymerase (adds nucleotides), Ligase (joins Okazaki fragments)
  • Leading strand: Synthesized continuously; Lagging strand: discontinuously (Okazaki fragments)
MCQ Insight (Q60): Double-stranded DNA produced but missing covalent bonds between nucleotides on lagging strand = DNA LIGASE is inhibited. Ligase joins Okazaki fragments. If primers were absent, replication wouldn't start. If DNA polymerase blocked, no new strand would form.
NOT Tested: Specific enzyme names beyond helicase, topoisomerase, primase, DNA polymerase, and ligase.
Topics 6.3–6.4 — Transcription & Translation
  • mRNA: Carries info from DNA to ribosome
  • tRNA: Carries amino acid; anticodon pairs with mRNA codon
  • rRNA: Structural component of ribosome
  • Transcription: RNA polymerase reads template DNA (3'→5') → synthesizes mRNA (5'→3')
  • Eukaryotic mRNA processing: Poly-A tail (stability) + 5' GTP cap (ribosome recognition) + intron excision/exon splicing (alternative splicing → multiple proteins from one gene)
  • In prokaryotes: Transcription and translation happen simultaneously (no nucleus)
  • Translation: Ribosome reads mRNA codons → tRNA brings amino acids → polypeptide chain grows
  • Start codon: AUG (methionine) — must memorize
  • Universal genetic code: Nearly all organisms use same code → evidence of common ancestry
MCQ Insight (Q57): Eukaryotic gene expressed in prokaryote → prokaryote CANNOT remove introns from pre-mRNA → protein made but has different amino acid sequence (from unspliced introns) → non-functional protein. Genetic code is the SAME; ribosomes exist in both.
NOT Tested: Memorizing the full genetic code (except AUG). Details and names of translation factors.
Topics 6.5–6.6 — Gene Regulation
  • Regulatory sequences: Stretches of DNA that interact with regulatory proteins to control transcription
  • Constitutive genes: Always expressed
  • Inducible genes: Expressed only when needed
  • Epigenetic changes: Reversible modifications of DNA or histones → affect gene expression without changing DNA sequence
  • Transcription factors: Bind to promoter or enhancer sequences → initiate transcription
  • Negative regulators: Bind DNA and block transcription
  • Prokaryote operons: Groups of genes coordinately regulated (inducible or repressible)
  • Small RNA molecules: Regulate gene expression (e.g., microRNAs)
  • Cell differentiation: All cells in an organism have the same DNA; differential gene expression determines cell type
MCQ Insight (Q19): Why do some cells make enzyme X but others don't? The cells transcribe and translate DIFFERENT COMBINATIONS of genes — NOT different genes. All somatic cells of an organism have the same genome. Gene expression is regulated, not the genome itself.
Topics 6.7–6.8 — Mutations & Biotechnology
  • Point mutation: One nucleotide substituted for another
  • Frameshift mutation: One or more nucleotides inserted or deleted → reading frame shifts
  • Nonsense mutation: Point mutation creates a premature stop codon → truncated protein (missing C-terminus)
  • Silent mutation: Nucleotide change has NO effect on amino acid sequence (due to redundancy of genetic code)
  • Mutations can be: Beneficial, detrimental, or neutral; not directed by environmental pressures
  • Aneuploidy (nondisjunction): Changes in chromosome number → altered gene expression levels
  • Horizontal gene transfer (prokaryotes): Transformation, transduction, conjugation, transposition → increases genetic variation
  • Gel electrophoresis: Separates DNA fragments by size — smaller fragments travel farther through the gel. (For nucleic acids, the charge-to-mass ratio is constant, so size determines migration distance. Used for DNA fingerprinting, PCR verification, and genetic analysis.)
MCQ Insight (Q53): Protein missing amino acids at carboxyl terminus = NONSENSE MUTATION — a codon changed to a STOP codon → ribosome stops early. Frameshift would shift all downstream codons, not just truncate. Histone wrapping prevents transcription entirely (no protein at all).
MCQ Insight (Q55): Extra chromosome 21 (trisomy) → more copies of genes on chr 21 → more mRNA transcribed → more protein produced → abnormal cell function. Not about physical crowding.
📚 Unit 6 Key Vocabulary
Central Dogma
The fundamental principle of molecular biology: DNA → RNA → Protein. Information flows from DNA (storage) to mRNA (messenger) to protein (functional product). Retroviruses reverse the first step.
DNA Replication
Semiconservative process that copies the entire genome before cell division. Each new double helix contains one original (template) strand and one newly synthesized strand. Occurs in the 5'→3' direction.
Semiconservative Replication
Each new DNA molecule consists of one original (parental) strand and one new strand. Ensures that genetic information is accurately copied and that each daughter cell gets a complete genome.
Helicase
Enzyme that unwinds and separates the two strands of the DNA double helix at the replication fork by breaking the hydrogen bonds between base pairs. Creates a template for DNA synthesis.
DNA Polymerase
Enzyme that synthesizes new DNA strands by adding nucleotides complementary to the template strand. Can only synthesize in the 5'→3' direction. Requires a primer (RNA) to start.
DNA Ligase
Enzyme that joins Okazaki fragments on the lagging strand by forming phosphodiester bonds between adjacent DNA fragments. "Seals" gaps in the sugar-phosphate backbone.
Okazaki Fragments
Short segments of DNA synthesized discontinuously on the lagging strand during replication. Later joined by DNA ligase. Result of DNA polymerase only synthesizing 5'→3'.
Transcription
Process of synthesizing mRNA from a DNA template. RNA polymerase reads the template DNA strand (3'→5') and synthesizes mRNA (5'→3'). Occurs in the nucleus of eukaryotes.
RNA Polymerase
Enzyme that synthesizes RNA by reading the template DNA strand. Unlike DNA polymerase, it does not need a primer. Binds to the promoter region to initiate transcription.
Promoter
DNA sequence upstream of a gene where RNA polymerase and transcription factors bind to initiate transcription. Determines when and how much a gene is expressed.
mRNA Processing (Eukaryotes)
Pre-mRNA modifications before export from nucleus: (1) 5' GTP cap added (ribosome recognition), (2) poly-A tail added (stability), (3) introns spliced out, exons joined (alternative splicing).
Intron
Non-coding sequence within a pre-mRNA that is removed (spliced out) before the mature mRNA is translated. Prokaryotes generally lack introns — key reason a eukaryotic gene expressed in a prokaryote produces abnormal protein.
Exon
Coding sequences of a pre-mRNA that are retained in the mature mRNA and translated into protein. Alternative splicing of exons allows one gene to produce multiple different proteins.
Translation
Process of synthesizing a polypeptide from mRNA codons, occurring at ribosomes. tRNA molecules bring specific amino acids based on anticodon-codon complementarity. Three stages: initiation, elongation, termination.
Codon
Three-nucleotide sequence on mRNA that specifies one amino acid or a stop signal. The genetic code uses 64 codons for 20 amino acids — most amino acids have multiple codons (redundancy/degeneracy).
Anticodon
Three-nucleotide sequence on a tRNA molecule that is complementary to an mRNA codon. Base-pairs with the codon during translation to deliver the correct amino acid to the growing chain.
Start Codon
AUG — the codon that initiates translation. Codes for methionine (the first amino acid in every new polypeptide). Must memorize this for the AP Exam.
Point Mutation
Single nucleotide substitution in DNA. May result in: same amino acid (silent/synonymous), different amino acid (missense), or premature stop codon (nonsense). Effects depend on context.
Frameshift Mutation
Insertion or deletion of nucleotides NOT in multiples of 3, shifting the reading frame. All codons downstream of the mutation are altered → usually drastically changes the protein.
Nonsense Mutation
Point mutation that converts an amino acid codon into a stop codon. Terminates translation prematurely → truncated protein missing its carboxyl terminus. Often results in loss of function.
Silent Mutation
Nucleotide change that does NOT alter the amino acid sequence (due to the redundancy of the genetic code). Has no effect on phenotype — the same protein is still produced.
Epigenetic Modification
Heritable change in gene expression that does NOT involve a change in DNA sequence. Examples: DNA methylation (often silences genes), histone acetylation (often activates genes).
Transcription Factor
Protein that binds to specific DNA sequences (promoters or enhancers) and either activates or represses transcription of a gene. Key mechanism of differential gene expression.
Operon
Prokaryotic gene regulatory system where multiple related genes are controlled by a single promoter and regulated together. Example: lac operon in E. coli — controlled by lactose availability.
PCR (Polymerase Chain Reaction)
Technique to amplify specific DNA sequences: (1) denature DNA, (2) anneal primers, (3) extend with DNA polymerase. Repeated cycles exponentially increase DNA copy number.
Gel Electrophoresis
Technique that separates DNA fragments by size using an electric current through a gel. Smaller fragments travel farther. Used for DNA fingerprinting, genetic analysis, and PCR verification.

Unit 7 — Natural Selection

The largest unit by exam weight (13–20%). Covers mechanisms of evolution, Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium, evidence for evolution, phylogenetics, and speciation. Write about populations evolving, NOT individuals.

Topics 7.1–7.3 — Natural & Artificial Selection
  • Natural selection: Acts on phenotypic variation; individuals with favorable phenotypes survive → reproduce more → pass traits to offspring
  • Fitness: Measured by REPRODUCTIVE SUCCESS (not just survival)
  • Requirements for evolution by natural selection: Heritable variation + differential reproductive success due to that variation
  • Environments create selective pressure: Different pressures favor different phenotypes
  • Artificial selection: Humans select for/against traits → changes allele frequencies in populations
Critical Language: "Populations evolve" — NOT "individuals evolve." Natural selection acts on individuals but changes the genetic makeup of POPULATIONS. Avoid Lamarckian statements like "organisms develop traits because they need them."
MCQ Insight (Q59): Poaching large-tusked elephants = artificial selection removing large-tusk alleles from population → future generations have smaller average tusk size. This is directional selection acting on a heritable trait.
Topic 7.4 — Population Genetics
  • Mutation: Random process; adds new genetic variation; source of all new alleles
  • Genetic drift: Random change in allele frequencies due to chance; significant in SMALL populations
  • Bottleneck effect: Dramatic reduction in population size → drastic loss of genetic diversity
  • Founder effect: Small group colonizes new area → allele frequencies reflect founding group, not original population
  • Gene flow: Migration moves alleles between populations; prevents populations from diverging into separate species
  • Changes in allele frequencies over time = evolution occurring
MCQ Insight (Q48): Green anoles show greater cold tolerance because individuals with MORE cold tolerance had greater reproductive success in colder areas (natural selection). Acquired traits are NOT inherited. Mutations are random — not directed by cold exposure.
Topic 7.5 — Hardy-Weinberg Equilibrium
  • Model for describing allele frequencies in a NON-EVOLVING population
  • 5 conditions for H-W equilibrium: Large population, no migration, no new mutations, random mating, no natural selection
  • These conditions are never truly met — useful as a null hypothesis
Hardy-Weinberg Equations
p² + 2pq + q² = 1
p + q = 1
p = frequency of dominant allele | q = frequency of recessive allele
p² = frequency of homozygous dominant (AA)
2pq = frequency of heterozygous carriers (Aa)
q² = frequency of homozygous recessive (aa) = frequency of affected individuals
MCQ Insight (Q11): Prevalence of disorder = q² (homozygous recessive). Find q = √(q²). Then p = 1−q. Carrier frequency = 2pq. Common mistake: confuse allele frequencies (p, q) with genotype frequencies (p², 2pq, q²).
Topics 7.6–7.7 — Evidence for Evolution & Common Ancestry
  • Fossil record: Dating by rock age, carbon-14 decay, geographical data; shows continuous change
  • Morphological homologies: Shared structural features (including vestigial structures) → common ancestry
  • Molecular evidence: DNA and protein sequence similarities → the more similar, the more recently related
  • Evidence of common ancestry of all eukaryotes: Membrane-bound organelles, linear chromosomes, genes with introns
MCQ Insight (Q42): More amino acid substitutions in cytochrome c = longer time since divergence = more distantly related. Insects and vertebrates diverged ~600 million years ago — most amino acid substitutions = most distantly related pair.
Topics 7.9–7.10 — Phylogeny & Speciation
  • Phylogenetic trees: Show amount of evolutionary change over time (calibrated by fossils or molecular clock)
  • Cladograms: Show relative relationships; NO time scale
  • Nodes: Represent most recent common ancestor of two groups
  • Outgroup: Most distantly related to all others in the tree
  • Shared derived characters: Traits present in multiple lineages indicate common ancestry
  • Molecular data: More reliable than morphology for constructing trees
  • Biological species concept: Species = group capable of interbreeding to produce VIABLE, FERTILE offspring
  • Allopatric speciation: Geographic isolation → populations diverge
  • Sympatric speciation: No geographic isolation; still become reproductively isolated
  • Prezygotic barriers: Prevent mating or fertilization (habitat, temporal, behavioral, mechanical isolation)
  • Postzygotic barriers: Hybrid offspring are inviable OR sterile
  • Punctuated equilibrium: Rapid evolution after long stasis; Gradualism: slow continuous change
  • Divergent evolution: New habitats → phenotypic diversification
  • Convergent evolution: Similar selective pressures → similar phenotypes in unrelated species
MCQ Insight (Q24): Strongest evidence for DIFFERENT species = produce viable but STERILE offspring (postzygotic barrier). DNA sequence differences and morphological differences exist WITHIN species too. Habitat differences don't define species.
MCQ Insight (Q32): Goats + sheep produce sterile hybrids = POSTZYGOTIC barrier (different chromosome numbers → no homologous pairs → cannot produce gametes). This maintains reproductive isolation.
MCQ Insight (Q50): Outgroup = species MOST DISTANTLY related to all others. In a cladogram, the outgroup branches from the base — the first to diverge from the rest.
Topics 7.11–7.12 — Variation & Origins of Life
  • Genetic diversity: Populations with more diversity are more resilient to environmental change
  • Low genetic diversity populations: At risk of decline or extinction
  • Heterozygote advantage: Heterozygous genotype has higher fitness than either homozygote
  • Mutations not directed: Mutations are random — not caused by need or environment
  • Earth's timeline: Earth formed ~4.6 bya; habitable ~3.9 bya; earliest life ~3.5 bya
  • RNA World hypothesis: RNA may have been earliest genetic material; RNA can self-replicate and catalyze reactions
📚 Unit 7 Key Vocabulary
Natural Selection
Mechanism of evolution: individuals with heritable traits that increase survival and reproduction in their environment leave more offspring → those alleles increase in frequency over generations.
Fitness
Measured by reproductive success — the number of offspring an organism contributes to the next generation relative to others. NOT just survival. A sterile organism has zero fitness.
Adaptation
An inherited trait that increases an organism's fitness in its environment. Adaptations are the product of natural selection acting on heritable variation over many generations.
Allele Frequency
The proportion of a particular allele among all alleles for a gene in a population. Measured as a decimal (0 to 1) or percentage. Changes in allele frequency = evolution.
Directional Selection
Natural selection favors one extreme phenotype over others, shifting the population mean in that direction. Example: antibiotic resistance — bacteria with resistance genes survive and reproduce.
Stabilizing Selection
Natural selection favors the intermediate phenotype, reducing variation. Extremes are selected against. Example: human birth weight — very small or very large babies have lower survival rates.
Disruptive Selection
Natural selection favors both extreme phenotypes over the intermediate, increasing variation. Can lead to two distinct phenotypic groups. Example: bill sizes in certain bird populations.
Genetic Drift
Random change in allele frequencies due to chance events, not selection. Most powerful in small populations. Can cause alleles to be lost or become fixed regardless of their adaptive value.
Bottleneck Effect
Form of genetic drift: a dramatic reduction in population size (from disaster, hunting, etc.) drastically reduces genetic diversity. The surviving population may not represent original allele frequencies.
Founder Effect
Form of genetic drift: a few individuals colonize a new area, carrying only a subset of the original population's genetic diversity. The new population's allele frequencies reflect the founders, not the source.
Gene Flow
Movement of alleles between populations through migration of individuals or gametes. Tends to reduce genetic differences between populations, homogenizing allele frequencies. Prevents speciation.
Hardy-Weinberg Equilibrium
A model describing allele and genotype frequencies in a non-evolving population. Five conditions: large population, no migration, no mutation, random mating, no natural selection. Useful as a null hypothesis.
Phylogenetic Tree
Branching diagram showing evolutionary relationships among organisms calibrated to time (based on fossils or molecular clock). Branch lengths can represent amount of evolutionary change.
Cladogram
Branching diagram showing hypothetical evolutionary relationships based on shared derived characters. Does NOT show time scale or degree of evolutionary change — only branching patterns.
Outgroup
The taxon that is most distantly related to all others in a cladogram or phylogenetic tree. Used as a reference point to root the tree and determine ancestral vs. derived character states.
Shared Derived Character
A trait shared by two or more groups of organisms that was present in their common ancestor but NOT in more distant ancestors. Used to infer common ancestry and build cladograms.
Molecular Clock
The observation that DNA/protein sequences accumulate mutations at roughly constant rates over time. Used to estimate when two species diverged from a common ancestor based on sequence differences.
Biological Species Concept
A species is a group of organisms capable of interbreeding and producing viable, fertile offspring. Reproductive isolation is the key criterion. Does not apply to asexual organisms or fossils.
Speciation
The evolutionary process by which new species arise from existing populations. Requires reproductive isolation — the prevention of gene flow between populations — allowing divergence over time.
Allopatric Speciation
Speciation that occurs when a population is separated by a geographic barrier, preventing gene flow. The isolated populations diverge and eventually become reproductively isolated even if reunited.
Sympatric Speciation
Speciation that occurs within the same geographic area, without physical barriers. Mechanisms include polyploidy (especially in plants), sexual selection, and habitat specialization.
Prezygotic Barrier
Reproductive isolation mechanism that prevents mating or fertilization before a zygote forms. Types: habitat, temporal, behavioral, mechanical, and gametic isolation.
Postzygotic Barrier
Reproductive isolation mechanism that acts after fertilization. Includes hybrid inviability (embryo doesn't develop) and hybrid sterility (offspring cannot reproduce). Example: mules (horse × donkey).
Convergent Evolution
Similar traits evolve independently in unrelated lineages in response to similar environmental pressures. Example: wings in birds, bats, and insects evolved independently.
Divergent Evolution
Related lineages evolve different traits as they adapt to different environments. Starting from a common ancestor, lineages branch into distinct forms. Leads to diversity within a group.
Adaptive Radiation
Rapid diversification of a lineage into many species, each adapted to different ecological niches. Often occurs when new habitats become available. Example: Darwin's finches on the Galápagos.
Heterozygote Advantage
The heterozygous genotype (Aa) has higher fitness than either homozygote (AA or aa). Maintains both alleles in the population. Example: sickle cell trait provides some malaria resistance.
RNA World Hypothesis
Proposes that RNA was the earliest genetic and catalytic molecule — before DNA and proteins. RNA can store information, self-replicate with base pairing, and catalyze reactions (ribozymes).

Unit 8 — Ecology

Brings all previous units together. Covers organismal responses, energy flow, population dynamics, community ecology, biodiversity, and ecosystem disruptions. Expect food web and trophic cascade questions.

Topic 8.1 — Organism Responses to Environment
  • Organisms respond to environmental changes via behavioral and physiological mechanisms
  • Organisms exchange information through visual, audible, tactile, electrical, and chemical signals
  • Cooperative behavior tends to increase individual fitness AND population survival
  • Behavioral responses affect fitness → contribute to population success
NOT Tested: Specific behavioral or physiological mechanisms; details of communication systems.
Topic 8.2 — Energy Flow Through Ecosystems
  • Endotherms: Use metabolic heat to maintain body temp; high metabolic cost
  • Ectotherms: Regulate temperature behaviorally (sunning, shade)
  • Energy flows through ecosystems; matter CYCLES between environment and organisms
  • Carbon cycle: Photosynthesis, cellular respiration, decomposition, combustion
  • Nitrogen cycle: N₂ fixation → ammonia → assimilation → ammonification → nitrification → denitrification; performed by microorganisms
  • Phosphorus cycle: Weathering releases phosphate; taken up by producers; returned by decomposition/excretion
  • Water (hydrologic) cycle: Evaporation, condensation, precipitation, transpiration
  • Trophic levels: Producers → primary → secondary → tertiary → quaternary consumers → decomposers; arrows show ENERGY FLOW direction
  • Autotrophs: Photosynthetic (sunlight) or chemosynthetic (chemical energy)
  • Heterotrophs: Carnivores, herbivores, omnivores, decomposers, scavengers
Common Error: Students draw food web arrows pointing in wrong direction. Arrows point FROM prey TO predator (in the direction energy flows), NOT toward the organism eating.
Topics 8.3–8.4 — Population Ecology
  • Population growth depends on birth rate, death rate, and population size
  • Carrying capacity (K): Maximum sustainable population given available resources
  • Density-dependent factors: Affect population in proportion to density (disease, competition, predation)
  • Density-independent factors: Affect population regardless of density (weather, natural disasters)
Population Growth Equations
Basic: dN/dt = B − D
Exponential: dN/dt = r_max · N
Logistic: dN/dt = r_max · N · (K−N)/K
N = population size | B = birth rate | D = death rate
r_max = maximum per capita growth rate | K = carrying capacity
Topic 8.5 — Community Ecology
  • Community: Groups of interacting populations of different species
  • Species diversity: Measured by species composition and diversity (Simpson's Diversity Index)
  • Predation: Predator/prey interactions → population dynamics
  • Mutualism (+/+): Both species benefit (e.g., pitcher plant and woolly bat)
  • Commensalism (+/0): One benefits; other unaffected
  • Parasitism (+/−): One benefits; other harmed
  • Competition (−/−): Both negatively affected; drives niche partitioning
  • Niche partitioning: Competing species use different resources or times to reduce competition
  • Trophic cascades: Changes in one trophic level have cascading effects on others
Simpson's Diversity Index
D = 1 − Σ(n/N)²
n = total individuals of a particular species | N = total individuals of all species
MCQ Insight (Q10): Pitcher plant provides shelter (bat benefits); bat provides nitrogen via feces (plant benefits) = MUTUALISM. Both parties benefit. Commensalism requires one to be UNAFFECTED — bat clearly benefits from shelter.
MCQ Insight (Q22): Bumblebees with different proboscis lengths in the same area use different flowers = NICHE PARTITIONING. They reduce competition by using different resources, not allopatric speciation (they live in the same area).
Topics 8.6–8.7 — Biodiversity & Disruptions
  • Ecosystems with MORE diversity: More resilient to environmental change
  • Keystone species: Disproportionate impact on ecosystem relative to abundance; removal causes ecosystem collapse
  • Invasive species: Exploit new niches free of predators/competitors; outcompete natives for resources
  • Disruptions: Global climate change, logging, urbanization, monocropping, invasive species all affect ecosystem structure
MCQ Insight (Q37): Volcanic activity reduces sunlight → less photosynthesis → producers decline → energy pyramid narrows → FEWER resources at each trophic level → DECREASE in most population sizes and number of trophic levels. More sunlight does NOT help omnivores avoid the impact.
MCQ Insight (Q58): Cadmium kills trout → fewer predators eating damselfly nymphs → nymph population INCREASES (trophic cascade). Tracing through the food web is key: Algae → Midge Larva → Damselfly Nymph → Trout.
📚 Unit 8 Key Vocabulary
Population
All individuals of the same species living in a defined geographic area at the same time. Members interact and potentially interbreed. The unit on which natural selection and genetic drift act.
Community
All populations of different species living and interacting in a defined area. Community structure is described by species composition and species diversity.
Ecosystem
A community plus its abiotic (non-living) environment. Energy flows through ecosystems (sun → producers → consumers → decomposers); matter cycles between biotic and abiotic components.
Autotroph (Producer)
Organism that produces its own food from inorganic sources. Photosynthetic autotrophs use sunlight; chemosynthetic autotrophs use chemical energy. Base of the food web; foundation of the energy pyramid.
Heterotroph (Consumer)
Organism that obtains energy by consuming organic molecules made by other organisms. Includes herbivores, carnivores, omnivores, decomposers, and scavengers.
Decomposer
Organisms (bacteria, fungi) that break down dead organic matter into inorganic components, returning nutrients to the environment. Essential for biogeochemical cycling.
Trophic Level
A step in the food chain; represents a group of organisms that share the same feeding position. Energy is lost (~90%) at each transfer between trophic levels, limiting the number of levels in an ecosystem.
Food Web
Complex network of feeding relationships in a community, showing who eats whom. Arrows indicate the direction of energy flow (from eaten to eater). More realistic than a linear food chain.
Primary Productivity
Rate at which autotrophs produce organic compounds (biomass) by photosynthesis or chemosynthesis. Determines the total energy available to all other organisms in the ecosystem.
Carrying Capacity (K)
The maximum population size an environment can sustainably support given available resources. When population reaches K, logistic growth rate slows to zero. Set by limiting factors.
Exponential Growth
Population growth that occurs when resources are unlimited — population doubles at a constant rate (J-shaped curve). Described by: dN/dt = r_max × N. Rare in nature for extended periods.
Logistic Growth
Population growth that slows as the population approaches carrying capacity (K) — produces an S-shaped curve. Density-dependent factors limit growth: dN/dt = r_max × N × [(K−N)/K].
Density-Dependent Factor
Limiting factor whose effect increases as population density increases. Examples: disease (spreads more easily in crowded conditions), competition for food, predation. Regulates population size.
Density-Independent Factor
Factor affecting population regardless of density. Examples: extreme weather, drought, volcanic eruption. Can cause sudden population crashes not related to resource competition.
Mutualism (+/+)
Symbiotic relationship where both species benefit. Example: nitrogen-fixing bacteria in legume root nodules (bacteria get carbon; plant gets fixed nitrogen); mycorrhizal fungi + plant roots.
Commensalism (+/0)
Symbiotic relationship where one species benefits and the other is unaffected. Example: barnacles on whale skin (barnacles get transport; whale is unharmed). Difficult to prove in practice.
Parasitism (+/−)
Relationship where one organism (parasite) benefits by harming the other (host). Parasite typically lives on/in the host. Example: tapeworms in intestines; ticks on mammals.
Niche Partitioning
Reduction of interspecific competition when species divide shared resources, using different aspects of the environment. Allows multiple species to coexist in the same habitat.
Keystone Species
Species with disproportionately large effect on its community relative to its abundance. Removal causes ecosystem collapse or dramatic reorganization. Example: sea otters controlling sea urchin populations.
Trophic Cascade
Indirect ecological interactions across trophic levels — a change at one level ripples through the food web. Example: removing top predators → prey population explodes → plants overgrazed → ecosystem collapses.
Invasive Species
Non-native species introduced (intentionally or accidentally) to a new environment, often thriving without natural predators and outcompeting native species. Can disrupt entire ecosystems.
Biogeochemical Cycle
Pathway by which a chemical element or molecule moves through both biotic (living) and abiotic (non-living) compartments of an ecosystem. Key cycles: carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus, water.
Nitrogen Fixation
Conversion of atmospheric N₂ gas into ammonia (NH₃/NH₄⁺) by nitrogen-fixing bacteria (e.g., Rhizobium in legume roots, Azotobacter in soil). Essential step making nitrogen available to plants.
Carbon Cycle
Global movement of carbon through photosynthesis (CO₂ → organic C), cellular respiration and decomposition (organic C → CO₂), and combustion of fossil fuels (stored C → CO₂).
Biodiversity
The variety of life in an area, measured as species richness (number of species) and species evenness (relative abundance). Higher biodiversity → greater ecosystem resilience and stability.
Simpson's Diversity Index
Quantitative measure of biodiversity: D = 1 − Σ(n/N)². A value closer to 1 indicates greater diversity. Accounts for both number of species and their relative abundances.
Endotherm vs. Ectotherm
Endotherms (mammals, birds) generate internal heat metabolically to maintain constant body temp. Ectotherms (reptiles, fish) rely on external heat sources and regulate temperature behaviorally.

Science Practices

All 6 science practices are assessed on both MCQ and FRQ sections. Questions can pair ANY content with ANY science practice. Know these skills — they are what differentiate a 3 from a 5.

1
Concept Explanation
  • 1.A — Describe biological concepts and processes
  • 1.B — Explain biological concepts and processes
  • 1.C — Explain in applied/real-world contexts
FRQ Tip: "Describe" = say what it is. "Explain" = say what AND why/how. Always use precise scientific vocabulary.
2
Visual Representations
  • 2.A — Describe characteristics of visual representations
  • 2.B — Explain relationships in biological models
  • 2.C — Explain how models relate to larger principles
  • 2.D — Represent relationships (diagrams, flowcharts)
Tip: Read figure captions carefully. Axes, legends, and error bars all contain information that the question may test.
3
Questions & Methods
  • 3.A — Identify or pose a testable question
  • 3.B — State null hypothesis or predict results
  • 3.C — Identify experimental procedures (IVs, DVs, controls)
  • 3.D — Propose new investigations based on evidence
Null Hypothesis: Must state that the INDEPENDENT VARIABLE has NO EFFECT on the DEPENDENT VARIABLE. Example: "The presence of light has no effect on the mass of grass."
4
Representing & Describing Data
  • 4.A — Construct appropriate graph type (bar, line, scatter, etc.)
  • 4.B — Describe data: specific points, trends, relationships
Graph Construction Checklist: ✓ Correct graph type (line for continuous data, bar for categorical) ✓ Axis labels with UNITS ✓ Appropriate scale ✓ Accurate data points ✓ Error bars when appropriate ✓ Trend line when appropriate
5
Statistical Tests & Data Analysis
  • 5.A — Mathematical calculations (equations, means, rates, ratios, percentages)
  • 5.B — Use confidence intervals/error bars to evaluate differences
  • 5.C — Perform chi-square hypothesis testing
  • 5.D — Use data to evaluate hypothesis; reject or fail to reject null
Error Bars (MCQ Insight Q7): Overlapping error bars (±2SE) = NOT statistically different. NON-overlapping error bars = statistically different. The earliest time point where error bars don't overlap = first statistically significant difference.
6
Argumentation
  • 6.A — Make a scientific claim
  • 6.B — Support claim with evidence from biology + data
  • 6.C — Provide reasoning connecting evidence to theory
  • 6.D — Explain relationship between results and larger concepts
  • 6.E — Predict causes/effects of change in biological system
CER Framework: Claim → Evidence → Reasoning. Every FRQ answer should have all three. Don't just state the claim and stop.

MCQ Exam Insights

Based on detailed analysis of three College Board exams: Practice Exam 1 (Spring 2020), Practice Exam 2 (Spring 2020), and the 2026 Supplemental Practice Exam MCQ — with full official answer rationales for all 60 questions each. These patterns reveal the reasoning skills the exam most frequently tests. Understanding WHY the answer is correct — and exactly WHY each wrong answer is wrong — is more valuable than memorizing answers.

Top Reasoning Patterns Tested (Across All 3 Exams)
Pattern 1 — Direction of Movement: Water moves from HIGH water potential → LOW (hypotonic → hypertonic). Molecules diffuse HIGH → LOW concentration. These directionality questions appear on every exam. Memorize both directions.
Pattern 2 — Mechanism Identification: Given a result, which mechanism caused it? Key clues: "cannot be overcome by more substrate" = allosteric; "missing bonds on lagging strand" = ligase; "sterile offspring" = postzygotic; "NADH buildup" = ETC blocked; "cells accumulate in S phase" = S-phase drug.
Pattern 3 — Natural Selection Language: Populations evolve, not individuals. Selection acts on EXISTING variation. Mutations are RANDOM — not directed by need. "Organisms needed the trait" = WRONG. "Individuals WITH the trait had higher fitness and reproduced more" = CORRECT every time.
Pattern 4 — Gene Expression vs. Genetic Change: Environmental changes to phenotype = gene EXPRESSION changes, NOT DNA mutation. Seasonal fur color, DNA methylation shifts, mRNA stability changes — all are epigenetic or regulatory, not mutations in the sequence.
Pattern 5 — Negative vs. Positive Feedback: Negative feedback REVERSES the original change (maintains homeostasis). Positive feedback AMPLIFIES it. Key test: does the response push back against the stimulus, or accelerate it? Ethylene ripening accelerates → positive. DMSP at high temps reduces further damage → negative.
Pattern 6 — Evidence Strength Hierarchy: Molecular/genetic evidence > morphological. For endosymbiosis: own DNA + independent division > folded membranes. For common ancestry: shared molecular sequences > body plan similarity. For disease causation: rescue experiment (introduce wild-type → disease corrected) > crossing experiments.
Pattern 7 — "Next Investigation" Questions: To understand a mechanism, identify the NEXT UNKNOWN step in the causal chain. If ROS activates JNK and JNK causes regeneration, the unknown is what in JNK pathway ROS activates — not what activated ROS (already known) and not what mol does (separate branch). Follow the arrow to the unknown.
Pattern 8 — Structure Determines Function: Mutations change primary structure (AA sequence) → change 3D shape → change function. Microvilli lost → lower SA:V → less absorption. Ribose vs. deoxyribose → RNA vs. DNA stability. Cristae folding → more ETC surface. This theme is tested in every unit.

Unit 1 — Chemistry of Life

2020 Exam 2 Q1 / 2026 Supp. Q59
Hydrolysis vs. Dehydration — Bonds Broken
Hydrolysis = ADDITION of water BREAKS covalent bonds between monomers (glycosidic bonds in starch, peptide bonds in proteins). Dehydration synthesis = REMOVAL of water FORMS bonds. Critical double-check: starch monomer bonds are COVALENT, not hydrogen. Hydrogen bonds hold double-stranded DNA together, not monomers in polymers.
2020 Exam 1 Q1
Specific Heat vs. Heat of Vaporization
Sweat cools the body via high heat of vaporization — evaporation absorbs heat from the skin's surface. High specific heat = water resists temperature change (stabilizes body temps and climate). Both arise from H-bonding, but they explain different phenomena. Don't conflate them.
2026 Supp. Q11
RNA vs. DNA Monomer — Ribose Is THE Key
Ribose (with –OH at the 2' carbon) is what defines an RNA nucleotide vs. DNA (deoxyribose). Both DNA and RNA contain nitrogen, phosphorus, and cytosine. Uracil is RNA-only, but only ribose appears in molecular diagrams as the structural differentiator. If you see "OH OH" on the sugar = ribose = RNA.
2026 Supp. Q15
Capillary Action Experiment — What Is Being Tested
Glass tubes of varying diameters in water test the effect of tube diameter on adhesion and cohesion. Adhesion = water sticking to glass (polar surface). Cohesion = water sticking to itself (H-bonds). Tube diameter does NOT affect water temperature, density, or the strength of covalent bonds within water molecules.
2026 Supp. Q35
Phospholipid Properties — Amphipathic
Phospholipids have both a polar phosphate head and nonpolar fatty acid tails (amphipathic). Common traps: they do NOT form chains of monomers (lipids aren't polymers via dehydration); they do NOT have amino or carboxyl termini (those are protein features); they do contain C–C bonds but also C–O and other bonds — not only C–C.
2020 Exam 1 Q22 / 2020 Exam 1 Q56
Bond Types in DNA — Which Breaks Where
UV radiation breaks hydrogen bonds between nitrogenous bases (causing thymine dimers). Covalent bonds in the sugar-phosphate backbone are strong — not broken by UV. Complementary DNA strands are held together by H-bonds (A=T: 2; G≡C: 3). The backbone within each strand = covalent phosphodiester bonds.

Unit 2 — Cell Structure & Function

2026 Supp. Q3
Secretory Pathway — Golgi, Not Ribosomes
Cells that secrete large amounts of enzymes (like salivary gland cells) need large Golgi complexes to modify and package enzymes into vesicles for exocytosis. The enzymes are made by BOUND ribosomes (on rough ER), not unbound ribosomes (those make cytoplasmic proteins). Mitochondria provide energy but don't directly aid digestion.
2026 Supp. Q12
Selective Membrane Permeability — Transport Proteins
Different cell types have different transport proteins embedded in their plasma membranes, which explains variation in membrane permeability. All plasma membranes are bilayers (always two layers — never one). Cell walls are external to membranes and don't affect which molecules cross the membrane.
2026 Supp. Q42–43
Microvilli → SA:V → Absorption
Intestinal microvilli increase the surface area-to-volume ratio of epithelial cells, providing more membrane surface for transport proteins → more nutrient and ion absorption. MVID (loss of microvilli) = lower SA:V = less absorption = watery intestinal contents. Ions (H⁺, Na⁺, Cl⁻) and large molecules (glucose, amino acids) require protein channels — they can't cross the lipid bilayer alone.
2026 Supp. Q44
MVID and Osmosis — Hypotonic Cytoplasm
In MVID, fewer ions are absorbed into cells → cytoplasm is more hypotonic than a healthy cell → water does NOT flow into the cell (water follows solutes) → more water stays in the intestinal lumen → watery waste. Common trap: students say "hypertonic" because fewer solutes were absorbed, but what matters is the relative concentration INSIDE the cell vs. the lumen.
2020 Exam 1 Q4 / 2026 Supp. Q37
Endosymbiosis — Two Separate Events in Plants
Plant cells have THREE types of DNA (nuclear, mitochondrial, chloroplast) because of TWO separate endosymbiotic events — one for mitochondria, one for chloroplasts. Strongest evidence: mitochondria and chloroplasts have their own DNA AND divide independently. Mitochondria did NOT evolve within a cell — they were free-living bacteria that were engulfed.
2020 Exam 1 Q48
Cristae Folding — Surface Area, Not Volume
Extensive folding of the inner mitochondrial membrane (cristae) increases surface area for ETC proteins and ATP synthase. It does NOT increase volume, provide insulation, or facilitate water diffusion across the outer membrane. More surface area = more ATP synthesis sites = more oxidative phosphorylation capacity.

Unit 3 — Cellular Energetics

2026 Supp. Q17
Ectotherms vs. Endotherms — Metabolic Rate
Ectotherms (oysters, reptiles) do NOT regulate body temperature — their body temp rises with environment → metabolic rate INCREASES because more enzyme-substrate collisions occur at higher temperatures. Endotherms maintain constant body temp. Common trap answer: "unaffected because ectotherms maintain homeostatic body temp" — WRONG, that describes endotherms.
2026 Supp. Q28 / 2020 Exam 1 Q3
Source of O₂ in Photosynthesis
Oxygen is produced by Photosystem II splitting water molecules (H₂O → 2H⁺ + ½O₂ + 2e⁻). O₂ is a byproduct of this water-splitting. Electrons from water are donated to PS II. Common wrong answers: mitochondria (they CONSUME O₂), CO₂ splitting (CO₂ is fixed in Calvin cycle, not split for O₂), lysosomes (unrelated).
2026 Supp. Q22
Light Reaction ETC — Electron and Proton Directions
ELECTRONS travel: PS II → ETC carriers → PS I → NADPH (stroma). PROTONS are pumped: stroma → thylakoid interior (building up inside). ATP is made when H⁺ flows back OUT through ATP synthase to the stroma. Key: electrons and protons move in opposite directions. Protons accumulate INSIDE the thylakoid; electrons move across the membrane via carrier proteins.
2026 Supp. Q41
Enzyme Free Energy Diagram — What Changes and What Doesn't
Enzymes lower activation energy but do NOT change the free energy of substrates or products. On a reaction coordinate diagram: the peak (transition state) is lower with enzyme, but the starting energy of substrates AND the ending energy of products are IDENTICAL in both catalyzed and uncatalyzed reactions. Enzymes don't make reactions more favorable — they just speed them up.
2026 Supp. Q51
Low pH and Enzyme Function
Acidic pH (lemon juice, H⁺) disrupts bonds between R groups of amino acids → alters 3D shape of active site → enzyme can't bind substrate. Acid does NOT: create new amino acid bonds, replace R groups (that would change primary structure), or change the covalent peptide bonds connecting amino acids. Primary structure is unchanged; tertiary structure is disrupted.
2026 Supp. Q58
Best Evidence for Proton Gradient Requirement
The most direct evidence that a proton gradient is required for ATP synthesis: increasing inner mitochondrial membrane permeability to protons DECREASES ATP production (gradient collapses → less H⁺ flows through ATP synthase). Other options (ETC exists in chloroplasts too; NADH depletion effects) are indirect. This rescue/disruption logic = strongest evidence type.
2020 Exam 1 Q5 / 2020 Exam 1 Q8
Location and Fate of Pyruvate
Krebs cycle = mitochondrial matrix. Glycolysis = cytosol. When O₂ is absent and pyruvate can't enter the Krebs cycle → fermentation produces lactic acid (in animals/bacteria) or ethanol + CO₂ (in yeast). Fermentation regenerates NAD⁺ to keep glycolysis running. Pyruvate is NOT produced by fermentation; it's the starting material for it.
2020 Exam 1 Q34
ETC Blockade → NADH Accumulates
Rotenone blocks Complex I of the ETC → NADH cannot be oxidized → NADH builds up dramatically. Glycolysis still occurs (it's in the cytosol and continues producing NADH). Other Krebs cycle products are less affected. High NADH = sign of ETC blockade, not Krebs or glycolysis blockade. This explains the rotenone experiment data pattern.

Unit 4 — Cell Communication & Cell Cycle

2026 Supp. Q5/8
Tissue Regeneration = Mitosis, Not Meiosis
To regenerate tissue (replace damaged cells), the body needs mitotic cell division = DNA replication (S phase) followed by mitosis and cytokinesis. Meiosis produces gametes, not replacement somatic cells. G₀ = non-dividing. G₁ slowing = less division. Continuous JNK activation → continuous mitosis → increasing cell division in the tissue.
2026 Supp. Q6
Positive Feedback in Cell Signaling Pathways
Tissue damage → ROS → activates JNK → activates mol expression → mol activates MORE ROS production → amplifies JNK further = positive feedback loop. Key indicator: the output of the pathway (mol expression) feeds back to increase the INPUT (ROS). This amplifies the regeneration signal rapidly. Negative feedback would oppose or reduce ROS.
2026 Supp. Q24
Reading Cell Cycle Histograms
In a cell cycle analysis histogram: G₁ = first peak (left); S phase = trough/broad region between peaks; G₂/M = second peak (right). Drug F-treated cells show a tall, broad S-phase peak = many cells arrested in S phase. Control cells show S phase as a low trough. If the drug accumulated cells in S, it likely blocked something needed to complete DNA replication or exit S phase.
2026 Supp. Q30
Cyclin Function in the Cell Cycle
Cyclins regulate progression through cell cycle stages by binding cyclin-dependent kinases (CDKs). Different cyclins are expressed at different stages. Common traps: cyclins do NOT bind DNA to stimulate replication (that's DNA polymerase), do NOT align chromosomes (that's the spindle), and do NOT bind cell surface receptors (that's signal transduction).
2020 Exam 1 Q10
Transcription Factor Behavior
A protein that moves from cytoplasm to nucleus after receiving a signal = a transcription factor. RNA splicing enzymes stay in the nucleus. Membrane proteins don't translocate to the nucleus. Hormones don't pool in the cytoplasm waiting to move. The nucleus-targeting behavior + gene regulation = transcription factor signature.
2020 Exam 2 Q12
Signal Cascade Amplification
Thickening arrows in a signaling cascade diagram = SIGNAL AMPLIFICATION. Each step activates many downstream molecules, so a small initial signal produces a large final response. This is NOT negative feedback (which would reduce the signal). This is a key function of G-protein cascades and kinase cascades — one molecule activates dozens at the next step.

Unit 5 — Heredity

2026 Supp. Q13
Chi-Square Expected Value Calculation
Expected value = (baseline % in absence of treatment) × (total sample size). If 10% of cells are normally in mitosis and you count 1,000 cells: expected = 1,000 × 0.10 = 100. Common mistakes: subtracting observed (926), subtracting the percentage difference (26), or multiplying 0.9 × 1,000 = 900. The expected value is always the CONTROL baseline applied to the experimental sample size.
2026 Supp. Q40
Chi-Square for Incomplete Dominance (1:2:1)
Incomplete dominance F2 cross → expected ratio is 1:2:1 (not 3:1). For 36 total offspring: 9 black, 18 gray, 9 white expected. Chi-square row for black: O=6, E=9, O−E=−3, (O−E)²=9, (O−E)²/E=1. Common trap: using 3:1 ratio (for dominant/recessive), or switching observed and expected in the formula.
2026 Supp. Q29
S Phase Before Meiosis — Chromosome Number
S phase before meiosis = DNA replication → each chromosome now has two sister chromatids → ensures gametes receive the correct number of chromosomes when chromatids separate. S phase does NOT ensure identical chromosomes to the parent (crossing over changes that). It ensures the chromosome count in gametes is haploid after meiosis I and II.
2026 Supp. Q31
Interpreting Knockout Data to Identify Gene Function
To identify which protein causes a phenotype using knockouts: compare each single mutant to the control. Only when Crs1 is knocked out does crossing over decrease (Cig1 knockout alone = no change; double Cig1+Crs1 = same decrease as Crs1 alone). Therefore Crs1 (not Cig1) facilitates crossing over. The logic: the gene whose absence causes the phenotype = the gene responsible.
2026 Supp. Q34
Linked vs. Unlinked Gene Probability
If leu and his are on separate chromosomes (unlinked) → independent assortment applies → P(leu⁺) = 1/2 AND P(his⁺) = 1/2 → P(leu⁺his⁺) = 1/4. When genes are LINKED (same chromosome), recombinant genotypes require crossing over and are much less frequent (as shown in the cyclin table). This contrast between linked and unlinked is a key exam concept.
2020 Exam 2 Q11 / 2020 Exam 1 Q18
Hardy-Weinberg: Carrier vs. Homozygous Recessive
Disorder prevalence = q². Find q = √(q²). Find p = 1−q. Carrier frequency = 2pq (heterozygous). Homozygous dominant = p². Most common mistake: confusing q² (affected) with 2pq (carriers). Also: never multiply q × q for heterozygotes — that gives q² again. Always use p + q = 1 to find the other allele frequency first.
2020 Exam 1 Q20
Mitochondrial Inheritance Pattern
Condition inherited by ALL offspring of affected MOTHERS regardless of sex = mitochondrial inheritance (maternally inherited). Y-linked = only affected XY individuals. X-linked dominant = affected fathers pass to ALL daughters. X-linked recessive = affected females require homozygous condition. Autosomal dominant = 50% of offspring of affected parent are affected. Mitochondrial = 100% from affected mothers, 0% from affected fathers.
2020 Exam 1 Q39 / 2020 Exam 2 Q40
Chi-Square Degrees of Freedom — Context Matters
H-W with 2 alleles: always df = 1 (critical value = 3.84 at p=0.05) — because knowing one allele frequency determines the other. Chi-square with 3 phenotype categories (incomplete dominance, non-H-W): df = 2 (critical value = 5.99). With 4 categories: df = 3 (critical value = 7.82). Count the number of categories, subtract 1. The degrees of freedom determines which critical value to use.

Unit 6 — Gene Expression & Regulation

2026 Supp. Q14
Coupled Transcription-Translation in Prokaryotes
In prokaryotes, transcription and translation occur simultaneously in the cytosol — ribosomes attach to the mRNA while it's still being transcribed. This is possible because prokaryotes have no nuclear membrane. In eukaryotes, transcription happens in the nucleus and translation in the cytoplasm — they are separated in both space and time (pre-mRNA processing occurs between them).
2026 Supp. Q18
DNA Methylation — Promoter Region
Methylation of the promoter region causes DNA to coil more tightly → transcription factors can no longer bind the promoter → RNA polymerase cannot initiate → mRNA synthesis is reduced or blocked. Key distinctions: methylation does NOT change the DNA sequence (epigenetic), does NOT affect ribosomes (translation), and does NOT alter splicing. It acts at the transcription initiation level.
2026 Supp. Q50
Transcription — Which Strand Is Read, What mRNA Looks Like
RNA polymerase reads the NONCODING (template) strand 3'→5' and produces mRNA that is complementary to it. The mRNA sequence is therefore IDENTICAL to the CODING strand — except U replaces T. Example: Noncoding: 5'-GTT AAC CTC GTA-3' → mRNA: 5'-CAA UUG GAG CAU-3'. Don't memorize just the rule — practice: given the noncoding strand, write the mRNA directly as the coding strand with U's.
2026 Supp. Q25
Mutation → Changed Shape → Changed Function (CFTR)
CFTR mutations change the amino acid sequence (primary structure) → altered 3D shape → channel no longer allows Cl⁻ passage. Mutations do NOT increase membrane lipid saturation (unrelated), do NOT make proteins hydrophobic (they're already embedded), do NOT cause clustering to admit other ions. Structure → function. This is the universal explanation for loss-of-function mutations in channel proteins.
2020 Exam 2 Q57
Eukaryotic Gene in Prokaryote — Intron Problem
Prokaryotes cannot remove introns from pre-mRNA → if a eukaryotic gene is expressed in a prokaryote, the resulting protein has a different (wrong) amino acid sequence because intron-derived sequences are translated → non-functional protein. The genetic code IS universal (same codons). Ribosomes exist in both. The problem is exclusively the lack of splicing machinery in prokaryotes.
2020 Exam 2 Q53
Truncated Protein = Nonsense Mutation
Protein shorter than expected (truncated at C-terminus) = nonsense mutation: a codon in the coding region mutated to a stop codon → ribosome stops early. Frameshift would alter ALL amino acids downstream. Histone-wrapping blocks transcription access — protein wouldn't be made at all. Different transcription factors affect when/where the protein is made, not its length.
2020 Exam 2 Q55
Extra Chromosome → More Protein
Trisomy (extra chromosome 21) → extra gene copies → more mRNA transcribed → higher protein levels for all genes on that chromosome. Does NOT cause crowding that blocks RNA polymerase. Does NOT affect other chromosomes. Does NOT interfere with mitosis (homologous pairing only matters in meiosis). The effect is straightforwardly quantitative: 3 copies → ~1.5× normal protein.
2020 Exam 2 Q60
DNA Ligase vs. Other Replication Enzymes
Double-stranded DNA formed but covalent bonds missing between Okazaki fragments on lagging strand = DNA ligase inhibited. If helicase blocked: no unwinding, no replication at all. If primase blocked: no primers, no initiation. If DNA polymerase blocked: no new strand synthesized. If hydrogen bonds disrupted: strands would separate. Ligase is specifically the SEALING enzyme — everything else works, but fragments aren't joined.

Unit 7 — Natural Selection & Evolution

2026 Supp. Q4
Derived vs. Ancestral Characters on Cladograms
Segmented BODIES in insects = ancestral character (shared with ancestors = inherited). Segmented HEADS and BRAINS = derived character (not in ancestors = evolved later in the insect lineage). A cladogram shows derived characters at the branch points where they arose. "Independently evolved" = a derived trait that appeared after the split, not a character shared from the common ancestor.
2026 Supp. Q36
Batesian Mimicry — Natural Selection on Existing Variation
A harmless species (Al. zaparo) that mimics a poisonous species (Am. bilinguis) evolved its coloration because individuals with similar coloration had reduced predation → higher survival → more reproduction = natural selection. Common wrong answers: they mated with the poisonous species (reproductive isolation prevents this between different species), or they "developed" the color during their lives (Lamarckian — acquired traits aren't inherited).
2026 Supp. Q49
Polar Bear Evolution — Selection on Pre-Existing Mutations
Polar bears evolved different LYST (pigment) and APOB (fat absorption) sequences because brown bears that happened to carry those mutations when they migrated to the Arctic had higher survival and reproduction. Mutations in these genes were random and pre-existing — NOT acquired in response to the Arctic (Lamarckian). NOT directed mutations. Natural selection then fixed these beneficial alleles in the population.
2026 Supp. Q23
Isolated Populations Evolve Differently
Ten fruit fly populations started from the same genetic stock but evolved different egg sizes in different directions. This shows that changing and different selective pressures in each isolated cage drove evolution in different directions. Common trap: "similar selective pressures = similar outcomes" — WRONG. Each population's microenvironment differed. Also WRONG: "mutations evolved for future needs" (Lamarckian).
2020 Exam 1 Q40
Genetic Bottleneck vs. Founder Effect
Genetic bottleneck = existing population dramatically reduced (e.g., hunting, disease) → survivors' gene pool is a random subset → reduced diversity. Founder effect = small group migrates away and starts a new population → low diversity because only a few founders' alleles are represented. Both cause reduced diversity, but the mechanism differs: one is a collapse; one is a departure.
2020 Exam 2 Q32
Prezygotic vs. Postzygotic Barriers
Goats and sheep can produce some offspring, but offspring are sterile → postzygotic reproductive barrier (barrier occurs AFTER fertilization). Prezygotic = prevents mating or fertilization entirely. If some zygotes ARE produced → postzygotic. Mismatched chromosome numbers → offspring can't pair chromosomes for meiosis → sterile offspring = postzygotic barrier.
2026 Supp. Q60
Genetic Drift from Bottleneck
90% of butterflies die from cold → population drastically reduced → allele frequencies change due to genetic drift (random chance, not selection). Surviving alleles are a random subset. Stress does NOT cause directed mutations to new alleles. Exponential growth after the event would change population size but NOT change allele frequencies. This is a textbook genetic drift/bottleneck scenario.
2020 Exam 1 Q50
Types of Natural Selection on Graphs
Stabilizing: middle phenotype favored, both extremes selected against → distribution narrows. Directional: one extreme favored → distribution shifts. Disruptive: both extremes favored, middle selected against → bimodal distribution. Predation from BOTH ends of the phenotypic spectrum → middle survives → stabilizing. Remember: the type of selection is determined by WHICH phenotypes have higher fitness, not just whether the population changes.

Unit 8 — Ecology

2026 Supp. Q1
Trophic Level Population Sizes — Energy Loss
Higher trophic level organisms have smaller populations because less energy is available to them — approximately 90% of energy is lost as heat at each trophic level (only ~10% transferred). They are not "better adapted to small populations" (misleading evolutionary framing). They do NOT require less energy — they require MORE energy per individual than organisms at lower levels.
2026 Supp. Q2
Population Growth Calculation
Population after one year = N₀ × (1 + r). For 218,000 deer at 3.7% growth: 218,000 × 0.037 = 8,066 added → 218,000 + 8,066 = 226,066. Common mistakes: multiplying by 4.7 instead of 1.037, or just reporting the growth (8,066 alone). The new population size = original + growth. This formula also applies to compound interest and exponential growth concepts.
2026 Supp. Q26
Simpson's Diversity Index
Simpson's D = 1 − Σ(n/N)² where n = count of each species and N = total organisms. For 7 oak + 3 maple (N=10): 1 − [(7/10)² + (3/10)²] = 1 − [0.49 + 0.09] = 1 − 0.58 = 0.42. D closer to 1 = more diverse; closer to 0 = less diverse. The area size (250 m²) is irrelevant — only species counts matter.
2026 Supp. Q27
Fire Ecology — Nutrient Release, Not Direct Energy
Fire improves seedling survival because it releases nutrients (N, P) from burned vegetation into the soil, making them available for seedling uptake through roots. Fire does NOT directly provide energy to seedlings, does NOT protect seeds, and does NOT leave small seedlings untouched (most vegetation burns). Nutrient release → reduced competition + more available resources = survival advantage.
2026 Supp. Q39
Mutual Predation — Both Benefit AND Are Harmed
Cod eat adult herring (cod benefits, herring harmed). Herring eat cod eggs and larvae (herring benefits, cod harmed). Therefore BOTH species simultaneously benefit AND are harmed by their interaction. This is "mutual predation" — distinct from standard predator-prey (one benefits, one harmed), mutualism (both benefit), or competition (both harmed).
2020 Exam 1 Q41–42
Keystone Species and Density-Independent Factors
Harmful algal blooms = density-INDEPENDENT factor (affect otter populations regardless of size). A keystone species (like sea otters) is one whose removal destabilizes the ENTIRE community — not just one species. Mutualism involves two species benefiting; keystone effects cascade through the whole food web. Density-DEPENDENT factors intensify as population grows (competition, disease, predation).
2020 Exam 2 Q22
Niche Partitioning — Coexistence Through Resource Division
Bumblebee species with different proboscis lengths use different flowers in the same area → niche partitioning (resource partitioning) allows multiple competing species to coexist. This is NOT mutualism (bees don't benefit each other). NOT geographic isolation (they share habitat). The competitive exclusion principle is avoided by each species exploiting slightly different resources.

Science Practice Insights

2026 Supp. Q9
"Next Investigation" — Follow the Causal Arrow
To determine the mechanism by which X causes Y, identify the NEXT UNKNOWN step in the causal chain. If ROS → JNK → regeneration is established, the unknown is WHAT in JNK is activated by ROS. Identifying transcription factors of mol (a different branch) or how tissue damage alters ROS (already known) would NOT answer the question. The next investigation must target the specific unknown link — not a known link, and not a different pathway.
2026 Supp. Q19 / 2026 Supp. Q38
Independent vs. Dependent Variables — Precise Definitions
Independent variable = what the researcher CONTROLS/MANIPULATES. In the oyster experiment: location where collected (subtidal vs. intertidal). Dependent variable = what is MEASURED as a result. In the cancer study: percentage of cells undergoing division. Common traps: methylation level = dependent (it's measured). Age of oysters = controlled, not the variable being tested. Concentration of pollutants = independent, not dependent.
2026 Supp. Q45
Best Experiment to Establish Gene-Disease Causation
To determine if a gene mutation SPECIFICALLY causes a disease: the strongest experiment = rescue experiment — introduce wild-type allele into mutant organisms and observe if phenotype normalizes. If introducing wild-type MYO5B corrects the MVID disease, the mutation is confirmed as the cause. Crossing wild-type × mutant only gives genotype data. Attaching tissue segments tests adjacent effects, not gene causation.
2020 Exam 2 Q7 / 2020 Exam 1 Q16
Error Bar Overlap → Statistical Significance
Error bars (±2SE) that do NOT overlap = statistically significant difference. Error bars that DO overlap = NOT statistically different. Always check each specific comparison independently — groups 3 and 4 may differ from each other even if both differ from groups 1 and 2. Find the EARLIEST time point where bars stop overlapping — that's when the difference first becomes significant.
2020 Exam 2 Q38
Writing Null Hypotheses
Null hypothesis = "The independent variable has NO EFFECT on the dependent variable." Name both variables explicitly. "Light has no effect on grass mass." Even predicting a NEGATIVE effect is still an alternative hypothesis — it states the IV does affect the DV, just in a specific direction. Null = zero effect. Alternative = any non-zero effect (directional or undirected).
2026 Supp. Q47
Reading "Remaining Bound" Graphs — Direction Is Inverted
In the PheX–Tyr experiment, the y-axis shows "percent with Tyr REMAINING BOUND" — HIGHER = worse at removing Tyr; LOWER = better at removing. Variant D stays at 0% → removed ALL Tyr (best). Variant A climbs to 75% → barely removed any (worst). This is a common graph-reading trap: the favorable outcome corresponds to the LOWER line, not the higher one. Always read axis labels carefully.
2026 Supp. Q54
Applying Error Bar Logic to Compare Two Groups
Migratory vs. nonmigratory monarch resting metabolic rates: error bars overlap → no statistical difference. Flight metabolic rates: bars don't overlap → significant difference (nonmigratory higher). Apply this logic separately to EACH comparison in the figure. Don't conclude both comparisons are the same just because they appear on the same graph. Read each pair of bars independently.
2020 Exam 2 Q6 / 2026 Supp. Q40
Chi-Square Calculation — The Formula and Common Errors
χ² = Σ (O − E)² / E. O = observed count, E = expected count (from the known baseline or theoretical ratio × total). Errors: (1) dividing by O instead of E; (2) subtracting in the wrong order; (3) using percentages instead of counts. For a dihybrid cross (9:3:3:1): E = ratio fraction × total. For incomplete dominance (1:2:1): E values differ — use 1/4, 2/4, 1/4 of total.
Words and Phrases That Eliminate Wrong Answers
If the answer says...It's likely WRONG because...
"organism developed/acquired the trait and passed it on"Lamarckian — acquired traits are NOT inherited. Only heritable DNA changes are passed to offspring.
"mutation occurred IN RESPONSE to the environment" or "organisms actively mutated"Mutations are RANDOM, not directed by need or environment. Selection acts after mutations exist.
"the cell produces different genes"All somatic cells have the SAME DNA; gene EXPRESSION differs between cell types, not the genes themselves.
"mitochondria break down glucose in bacteria"Bacteria lack mitochondria. They use fermentation, glycolysis, or membrane-based ETC in their cytoplasm/membrane.
"only animals perform cellular respiration"ALL living organisms perform cellular respiration — plants, fungi, bacteria, archaea all included.
"enzyme is used up in the reaction"Enzymes are CATALYSTS — released unchanged after each reaction. They are NOT consumed.
"inhibition cannot be overcome by substrate" + answer says "competitive"Competitive inhibition CAN be overcome by more substrate (same binding site). If more substrate doesn't help → allosteric.
"mitochondria evolved within the cell"Mitochondria were incorporated via ENDOSYMBIOSIS — originally free-living bacteria engulfed by a host cell.
"convergent evolution explains shared traits in closely related species"Convergent evolution applies to DISTANTLY related species with similar selective pressures. Closely related shared traits = common ancestry.
"algal blooms are caused by climate change / warming"Harmful algal blooms are caused by NUTRIENT POLLUTION (excess N and P), not temperature change. Ocean warming → nonnative species introductions.
"prokaryote expresses eukaryotic gene to produce correct protein"Prokaryote CANNOT remove introns → wrong AA sequence → non-functional protein. Genetic code is universal; splicing is not.
"NADH builds up = glycolysis is blocked"NADH buildup = ETC is blocked (can't oxidize NADH). Glycolysis continues producing more NADH that can't be processed.
"genetic bottleneck = a small group migrates to found a new population"That's the FOUNDER EFFECT. Genetic bottleneck = drastic reduction in an EXISTING population.
"ectotherms maintain homeostatic body temperature"ENDOTHERMS maintain homeostatic body temp. Ectotherms' body temperature varies with the environment.
"free energy of products differs between enzyme-catalyzed and uncatalyzed reactions"Enzymes lower activation energy only — both substrates AND products have identical free energy regardless of enzyme presence.
"hydrolysis breaks hydrogen bonds between monomers" (for polysaccharides/polypeptides)Hydrolysis breaks COVALENT bonds (glycosidic bonds, peptide bonds). Hydrogen bonds hold DNA strands together, not polymer backbones.
"the heat of fire directly provides energy to seedlings"Fire improves seedling survival by releasing NUTRIENTS (N, P) from burned organic matter into soil — not by providing direct energy.

• Bozeman = most AP-specific content alignment with CED
• Amoeba Sisters = hard-to-visualize processes (osmosis, meiosis, transcription)
• Crash Course = big-picture understanding fast
• Khan Academy = targeted topic + immediate practice
• HHMI = stunning animations of molecular processes
• Prof. Dave = in-depth mechanistic explanations
⏱ Suggested video-to-study ratio:
• Watch: 8–12 min video on one topic
• Pause at 3–4 min marks to recall
• After: write 3 things you learned from memory
• Then: do 3–5 practice questions on that topic
• Finally: revisit the unit content in this guide
⚠️ What NOT to do:
• Don't watch a 45-minute "full exam review" and call it studying
• Don't rewatch the same video — move to practice instead
• Don't skip Bozeman Science — it's the most AP-aligned channel and aligns directly with the CED
🎯 AP Exam-specific prep:
• College Board AP Classroom has released FRQs from past years
• Practice drawing graphs (graphing is tested explicitly in Section II)
• Write out full CER responses, not just mental answers
• Know ALL required equations by heart — they appear on the exam
